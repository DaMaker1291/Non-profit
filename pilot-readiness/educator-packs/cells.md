## The question this review answers

Not "is the interface pleasant". **Would you use this to teach the concept, and what would you change before using it with your pupils?** Correct the subject accuracy first; then the level, the wording, and the model's simplifications.

## 1. What the learner is meant to be able to do

Explain what each cell part does by what stops working without it, and say why a plant cell can lack chloroplasts.

**The prediction the learner must commit to before touching the model:** A root hair cell is a plant cell with no chloroplasts. Describe what it can no longer do, and what it can still do.

**Describe prediction — nothing is marked.** Prompt: Two sentences: what the missing part stops, and where the energy it still needs comes from.

**Ideas the learner can check their own sentence against:**

- that it can still release energy from glucose
- that the glucose now has to arrive from somewhere else
- that a part stopping its job is not the cell dying

**What the reveal says once the description is committed:** The model shows the jobs that stop when a part is switched off: without chloroplasts the cell cannot make glucose from light, while every other part keeps working. Mitochondria are still there, so it can release energy from glucose that the leaves made and sent down to the root. Losing one part is not the same as the cell dying.

**What the learner does with it afterwards:** Switch a part off and say what will stop working before you read the sentence.

**Starting point the lesson assumes:** A brick wall and a living factory look equally solid from a distance, and only one of them is doing anything. A cell is the smallest piece of a living thing that can carry out all the processes of life: it takes in materials, releases energy, makes what it needs and gets rid of waste. Most cells are too small to see without a microscope, which is why the idea feels abstract until you look at one.

## 3. Design decision

Structure is taught as the job it does, and switching a part off says what stops — never 'the cell dies'. The cell type is switched as a whole set (a plant cell is not an animal cell plus extras, and learners add animal-only parts to plant cells as well as the reverse), and the model's own gap — that not every plant cell has chloroplasts — is printed beside it rather than hidden.


### Misconceptions the lesson is built to refute

- **plant-is-animal-plus-extras** — A plant cell is an animal cell with a wall and chloroplasts added. _(eric-2015-draw-write-cells)_
- **chloroplast-in-every-plant-cell** — Every plant cell has chloroplasts. _(apfslt-2009-cell-teaching)_
- **ribosome-is-powerhouse** — The ribosome is the cell's energy producer. _(atlantis-2021-organelle-misconceptions)_
- **chlorophyll-is-the-organelle** — Chlorophyll is the organelle where photosynthesis happens. _(eric-2025-photosynthesis-misconceptions)_

## 4. The model: assumptions and limits

**Intended level:** 11–14 (cell structure and organelle function)

**Assumptions (scientific/mathematical):**

- One generic plant cell and one generic animal cell stand for all cells of that type.
- Seven organelles are shown; others (Golgi, ER, lysosomes) are omitted at this level.
- Each organelle is given one job, though most have several.
- Switching a part off describes what stops, not what kills the cell.

**What it cannot show:**

- NO CELL-TYPE DIMENSION: root hair, storage and leaf cells differ, and not every plant cell has chloroplasts. The model states this limitation in its copy rather than showing it.
- No scale, no tissue context, no specialised animal cells.
- Prokaryotes are not represented at all.

**Misconceptions it addresses:**

- A plant cell is an animal cell with a wall and chloroplasts added.
- Only animal cells have mitochondria.
- The ribosome is the powerhouse of the cell.
- Chlorophyll is the organelle where photosynthesis happens.

**Misconceptions it might accidentally introduce — please check these first:**

- That every plant cell has chloroplasts, which the limitation note contradicts in words but the picture does not.
- That a cell dies when one organelle stops, which the failure sentences deliberately avoid saying.
- That organelles are drawn to scale relative to one another.

## 6. The teaching sequence

_This plan has no authored sections; the page groups its idea paragraphs. Treat the sequence as unreviewed structure._

A cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. Every living organism is made of one cell (a bacterium) or many cells (a plant, an animal, you). The cell is not a container with parts floating in it by accident: each part, or organelle, has a job, and the shape of the part is tied to that job.

An animal cell has a cell membrane around the outside, cytoplasm inside, a nucleus, and mitochondria. The membrane is a thin, flexible boundary that controls what enters and leaves. The cytoplasm is where many reactions happen. The nucleus holds the genetic material (DNA) and controls the cell's activities. Mitochondria are where aerobic respiration releases energy from glucose.

A plant cell has the same four parts and three more that matter here: a cellulose cell wall outside the membrane, chloroplasts, and a large permanent vacuole. The wall is strong and gives the cell its shape; it is freely permeable to water and small dissolved substances, but it is not the selective barrier — the membrane underneath is. Chloroplasts contain chlorophyll and carry out photosynthesis. The vacuole is filled with cell sap and helps keep the cell firm.

Specialised cells show the same rule at a larger scale: structure serves function. A red blood cell has no nucleus and is packed with haemoglobin, leaving more room to carry oxygen. A root hair cell has a long, thin extension that increases the surface area for absorbing water and minerals. A sperm cell has many mitochondria to power its journey. A nerve cell is long so it can carry an electrical impulse from one part of the body to another.

Cells are not all the same size, and the units matter. Most plant and animal cells are measured in micrometres (μm): one micrometre is one-thousandth of a millimetre, or 10⁻⁶ m. A typical cheek cell is about 50–100 μm across. Magnification tells you how many times larger the image is than the object; it does not, by itself, tell you how much detail you can resolve.

Similar cells group into tissues, tissues into organs, and organs into organ systems. This hierarchy is why a single cell does not need to do everything in a large organism: specialised cells do one job well, and the system coordinates them. It also explains why a large organism cannot simply be one giant cell — the surface area available for exchange grows more slowly than the volume that needs supplying.

## 7. Questions to ask and traps to expect

- **"Plant cells have no mitochondria because they get energy from sunlight."** Photosynthesis makes glucose; respiration releases energy from glucose, and plant cells carry out respiration day and night. Mitochondria are therefore present in plant cells too. Sunlight is the energy source for making the glucose, not a replacement for respiring it.
- **"The cell wall controls what enters the cell."** The cellulose wall is strong and freely permeable to water and small dissolved substances: it gives shape and support, but it is not the cell's selective barrier. The cell membrane underneath is the selectively permeable boundary that controls what enters and leaves.
- **"A red blood cell has a nucleus like every other cell."** A mature human red blood cell loses its nucleus during development. That leaves more space for haemoglobin, so each cell can carry more oxygen. The absence is a specialisation, not a mistake in the diagram.

**Recap the learner should be able to say:**

- A cell is the smallest unit that carries out all the processes of life; its parts are organelles with specific jobs.
- Animal and plant cells share a membrane, cytoplasm, nucleus and mitochondria; plant cells also have a wall, chloroplasts and a large vacuole.
- Specialised cells have shapes that increase the surface area or machinery needed for their particular job.

## 8. Teacher run-of-show

### Before anyone touches the model

1. Show the plant cell and switch the chloroplast off. Ask what the cell can no longer do, and then what it can still do.
1. Switch the wall off and ask why the shape changes while the cell does not die.

### Questions to ask while it runs

- Which parts do both cell types have, and why would both need them?
- What is the difference between a part stopping its job and the cell dying?

### One guided activity

Pairs are given four cell types (root hair, leaf palisade, animal muscle, bacterial) and must tick the parts each has, then explain one absence.

### With no device

Two blank cell outlines to label, and a table of parts against cell types with one 'why it is absent' column.

### What to look for

Listen for 'the plant cell is an animal cell plus a wall and chloroplasts'. The correction is the vacuole and the absence of chloroplasts in root cells, not a longer list.

### Where to go next

- Specialised cells and their shapes.
- Photosynthesis and respiration compared.
