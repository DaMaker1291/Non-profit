## The question this review answers

Not "is the interface pleasant". **Would you use this to teach the concept, and what would you change before using it with your pupils?** Correct the subject accuracy first; then the level, the wording, and the model's simplifications.

## 1. What the learner is meant to be able to do

Add two fractions with different bottoms by making the pieces the same size first, and say why the bottoms are never added.

**The prediction the learner must commit to before touching the model:** A learner works out 1/2 + 1/3 by adding tops and bottoms and writes 2/5. Is 2/5 bigger than 1/2, smaller, or the same?

- Bigger — you added something to it
- **Smaller — 2/5 is less than a half** *(the answer the model shows)*
- The same — the method is valid

**What the reveal says once a choice is made:** Smaller: 2/5 is 0.4 and 1/2 is 0.5. Adding a positive amount cannot make a number smaller, so 2/5 cannot be the sum — the method has produced an answer that is impossible, not merely inaccurate. Renaming both fractions in sixths gives 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6.

**What the learner does with it afterwards:** Change one fraction and say which common denominator you expect before you check.

**Starting point the lesson assumes:** Two people both eat some pizza. One eats half of a pizza, the other eats a third of a pizza the same size. Together they have not eaten two fifths of anything: a half and a third are counts of DIFFERENT-SIZED pieces, and until both are counted in the same size, there is no number to add. That is the whole method, and the only reason a common denominator appears at all — it is the unit you agree to count in.

## 3. Design decision

The bottom number is never added, and the learner is given a test they can run themselves rather than a rule to memorise. The model renames both fractions in the smallest shared piece size, shades the sum, and draws the 'add the tops and the bottoms' answer to its own scale beside it — so the wrong answer is visibly smaller than one of the numbers being added, which no sum of two positive fractions can be. The correct route is an observation about piece size, not an algorithm announced in advance.


### Misconceptions the lesson is built to refute

- **add-tops-and-bottoms** — 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5, by adding the tops and the bottoms. _(ejmste-2022-fraction-misconceptions)_
- **multiply-rule-for-addition** — The multiplication rule (multiply tops, multiply bottoms) also adds fractions. _(ejceel-2024-fractions-basic7)_
- **denominator-as-quantity** — The denominator is how many pieces there are, so it is a quantity to add. _(newstead-murray-1998-fractions)_

## 4. The model: assumptions and limits

**Intended level:** 11–14 (adding fractions with unlike denominators)

**Assumptions (scientific/mathematical):**

- Both fractions are positive and proper, with denominators from 2 to 12.
- The smallest common denominator is used, though any common multiple would be valid.
- Addition is represented as areas of equal-sized pieces of the same whole.
- The 'add tops and bottoms' answer is the mediant, drawn at its own scale.
- Simplification uses the greatest common divisor only.
- Only two addends are modelled.

**What it cannot show:**

- No subtraction, multiplication or division of fractions.
- No mixed numbers or improper fractions.
- No estimation or number-sense check beyond the impossible-sum test.
- Only two addends, so a chain of additions is not shown.

**Misconceptions it addresses:**

- 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5 by adding tops and bottoms.
- The multiplication rule also adds fractions.
- The denominator is a quantity to be added.

**Misconceptions it might accidentally introduce — please check these first:**

- That a sum is always checked by an area diagram, which will not always be available.
- That the impossible-sum test always works: when the two fractions are equal, the mediant equals them, so the test cannot reject the wrong answer. The model's sentence handles this by falling back to the piece-size explanation.
- That the lowest common denominator is compulsory rather than convenient.

## 6. The teaching sequence

_This plan has no authored sections; the page groups its idea paragraphs. Treat the sequence as unreviewed structure._

The bottom number names the size of the piece being counted. 3/4 is "three quarters"; 2/3 is "two thirds". Adding them is asking for a count of pieces you have not yet chosen a size for, which is why the rule that follows is about converting and not about arithmetic.

Any two fractions can be counted in the same size of piece, because multiplying the top and the bottom of a fraction by the same number renames the pieces without changing the amount: 1/2 = 3/6 = 50/100. The smallest shared size is convenient, not compulsory — the product of the two bottoms always works, and is what to do when the smallest is not obvious. For 2/3 and 5/6 the shared size is sixths: 2/3 = 4/6.

Once the pieces match, the addition is the tops: 4/6 + 5/6 = 9/6. Nine sixths, which simplifies to 3/2, or 1 1/2. The bottom is not part of the addition because it is not a quantity — it is the name of the piece, and adding names is how 2/3 + 5/6 becomes 7/9, an answer smaller than 5/6, one of the numbers being added.

That size check is what makes the wrong answer visible instead of plausible. Adding two positive fractions must give MORE than either one: 1/3 + 1/4 is more than 1/3, so 2/7 cannot be right — 2/7 is smaller than 1/3. Before writing an answer down, ask whether it is the right sort of size; it catches the denominator slip without any arithmetic at all.

Subtraction uses the same method, and the same check in reverse: 3/4 − 2/3 = 9/12 − 8/12 = 1/12, which is less than 3/4 and more than nothing. A difference bigger than the number you started with is a sign that the pieces were never made the same size.

Mixed numbers are added in two parts — the wholes, then the fractions — and the fraction part still needs a common denominator: 2 1/2 + 1 1/3 = 3 + (3/6 + 2/6) = 3 5/6. The answer is then simplified if it can be, and only at the end: 9/6 was 3/2 all along, and simplifying mid-calculation is how a learner loses a factor they needed.

## 7. Questions to ask and traps to expect

- **"2/3 + 5/6 = 7/9 — add the tops, add the bottoms."** Adding the bottoms changes the size of the piece in the answer, so the sum is no longer about the same whole. The size check settles it without argument: 7/9 is smaller than 5/6, and adding 2/3 to 5/6 cannot make it smaller. Convert to sixths first: 4/6 + 5/6 = 9/6.
- **"2/3 + 5/6 = 7/6 — keep the bigger bottom and add the tops."** Sixths is the right unit, but 2/3 was never converted into it: two thirds is four sixths, not two sixths. Keeping the denominator without rescaling the fraction quietly adds a smaller number than the question asked for. Nine sixths is the answer, and it is 1 1/2 rather than 1 1/6.
- **"2 1/2 + 1 1/3 = 3 2/5 — add the wholes, then the fractions."** The whole parts may be added directly, but the fraction parts are counted in halves and thirds, so they need a shared size first: 3/6 + 2/6 = 5/6, giving 3 5/6. The 2/5 comes from adding halves to thirds as if the bottoms were quantities — the same slip as 1/2 + 1/3 = 2/5.

## 8. Teacher run-of-show

### Before anyone touches the model

1. Write 1/2 + 1/3 on the board and ask for an estimate first: 'is the answer more than one half?'. Every learner should say yes before any method appears.
1. Draw both fractions as bars cut in sixths so the pieces can be counted in the same unit.

### Questions to ask while it runs

- Why can we not add the bottoms? What would the bottom number be measuring?
- Is there a sum of two positive fractions that is smaller than one of them? Why not?

### One guided activity

Pairs sort six cards — three true sums, three made by adding tops and bottoms — into 'possible' and 'impossible', and must give a reason that does not involve calculating.

### With no device

Two bar diagrams per question cut into twelfths, with space to rename each fraction and write the sum.

### What to look for

Listen for 'a sum is bigger than its parts' as the check. A learner who gets the right answer by a remembered procedure but cannot reject 2/5 for 1/2 + 1/3 has not yet got the reason.

### Where to go next

- Subtracting fractions with different denominators.
- Mixed numbers and improper fractions.
