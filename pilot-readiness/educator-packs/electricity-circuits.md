## The question this review answers

Not "is the interface pleasant". **Would you use this to teach the concept, and what would you change before using it with your pupils?** Correct the subject accuracy first; then the level, the wording, and the model's simplifications.

## 1. What the learner is meant to be able to do

Predict what adding a cell or a bulb does to the current, and explain it with V = IR rather than with 'more power'.

**The prediction the learner must commit to before touching the model:** One cell and one bulb. You add a second identical bulb in series. What happens to the current?

- It stays the same — the cell is unchanged
- **It halves — the circuit's resistance doubles** *(the answer the model shows)*
- It doubles — there are more bulbs to carry it

**What the reveal says once a choice is made:** It halves. A cell holds a nearly fixed potential difference, not a fixed current; adding an identical bulb doubles the resistance and I = V/R falls to half. That is why the second bulb is dimmer than the first was — energy is taken from the charge, not charge itself.

**What the learner does with it afterwards:** Before adding anything, say what you expect the ammeter to read and why; then check.

**Starting point the lesson assumes:** A circuit is a closed path with a pump, a flow and a narrow place. The battery is the pump that gives charge energy; current is the rate at which charge flows; resistance is the narrowness that turns some of that energy into heat. The analogy is useful only up to a point — charge is not water, and it is not used up as it goes round — so the lesson replaces the picture with the quantities as soon as they are defined.

## 3. Design decision

Teach the loop before the formula. The model shows two readings of ONE loop, so the series-current rule is an observation rather than a definition, and every control the learner has (cells, bulbs, switch) changes both readings together. V = IR then arrives as the arithmetic of what they just watched.


### Misconceptions the lesson is built to refute

- **current-used-up** — Current is consumed by a bulb, so less reaches the next one. _(tused-2007-simple-circuits)_
- **battery-constant-current** — A cell supplies a fixed current regardless of the circuit. _(physrevper-2024-conceptual-resources)_
- **clashing-currents** — One wire from the cell is enough to light a bulb. _(pacificu-2006-electricity-misconceptions)_
- **sequential-reasoning** — What comes before the bulb in the loop matters more than what comes after. _(tused-2007-simple-circuits)_

### Where the sources disagree

- **Does 'substance' language about current (flowing, using up) predict the used-up error?** The 2007 study reports the error with frequencies; the 2024 study finds substance-based reasoning is often productive and not predictive of it. The model therefore corrects a PREDICTION (the two readings) instead of policing vocabulary. _(tused-2007-simple-circuits, physrevper-2024-conceptual-resources)_

## 4. The model: assumptions and limits

**Intended level:** 11–16 (series circuits, first quantitative treatment)

**Assumptions (scientific/mathematical):**

- Every cell is an ideal 1.5 V source with no internal resistance.
- Every bulb is identical at 6 Ω and its resistance does not change with temperature.
- The circuit is series only: one loop, no junctions.
- Wires and the closed switch have zero resistance.
- Brightness is proportional to power in the bulb relative to a one-cell, one-bulb reference.
- The arrows show CONVENTIONAL current: out of the positive terminal, round the outside loop, into the negative. Electron drift is the opposite way and is described in words, not drawn.

**What it cannot show:**

- No parallel branches, so local reasoning at a junction is not taught or refuted here.
- No non-identical bulbs, so 'the dimmer bulb has more resistance' cannot be explored.
- No internal resistance, so the voltage across the cells never sags as current rises.
- Real filament resistance rises with temperature; the model's current is a first approximation.
- No charge carriers are animated, so the electron drift can only be read in the disclosure under the drawing.

**Misconceptions it addresses:**

- Current is used up by a bulb.
- A cell supplies a fixed current whatever the circuit.
- One wire from a cell is enough to light a bulb.
- Where the switch sits in the loop changes the brightness.

**Misconceptions it might accidentally introduce — please check these first:**

- That brightness always follows (cells ÷ bulbs)², which is only true for identical bulbs in series.
- That all circuits are series circuits, because no parallel case appears.
- That 0.25 A is a measured value rather than an idealised prediction from stated assumptions.
- That the arrows show the electrons moving, when they show conventional current the other way.
- That current 'belongs' to a sign of charge rather than being a flow of charge whose direction is a convention.

## 6. The teaching sequence

_This plan has no authored sections; the page groups its idea paragraphs. Treat the sequence as unreviewed structure._

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge: I = Q/t, measured in amperes (A), where one ampere is one coulomb per second. Conventional current is drawn from the positive terminal to the negative terminal, even though electrons move the other way. An ammeter measures current and is connected in series, because the same current must pass through it and the component.

Potential difference, or voltage, is the energy transferred per unit charge between two points: V = W/Q, measured in volts (V). One volt is one joule per coulomb. A voltmeter measures the difference across a component and is connected in parallel with it, because it must compare the two sides without interrupting the current.

Ohm's law says that, at constant temperature, the current through a metallic conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference across it: V = IR. Resistance R is measured in ohms (Ω) and depends on the material, length, cross-sectional area and temperature. A longer wire has more resistance; a thicker wire has less; a hotter metal usually has more.

In a series circuit there is one path, so the current is the same through every component. The potential differences add to the supply voltage, and the total resistance is the sum: R = R₁ + R₂ + … . Adding a resistor in series always increases the total resistance and reduces the current.

In a parallel circuit the components are connected across the same two points, so the potential difference is the same across each branch. The currents add to the total, and the total resistance is found from 1/R = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + … . The total resistance is less than the smallest branch resistance, because the extra branch gives charge another path.

Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer: P = VI = I²R = V²/R. A resistor converts electrical energy to heat, which is the heating effect used in a filament lamp, a kettle and a fuse. The same power can be delivered at a lower current by using a higher voltage, which is why electricity is transmitted at high voltage and low current: less energy is lost as heat in the cables.

## 7. Questions to ask and traps to expect

- **"The current is used up as it goes round the circuit."** Charge is conserved: the same current enters and leaves a component in series. What is transferred is energy, not charge. A bulb does not consume current; it transfers electrical energy to light and heat, and the current continues.
- **"Two resistors in parallel have a total resistance of R₁ + R₂."** The series rule adds resistances because the same current passes through both. In parallel, each branch has the same voltage and the currents add, so the total resistance is smaller than either branch — for two 6 Ω resistors it is 3 Ω, not 12 Ω. Adding a branch makes it easier for charge to flow.
- **"A voltmeter goes in series with the component."** A voltmeter has a very high resistance and is connected in parallel, so it measures the potential difference across the component without diverting much current. An ammeter has a very low resistance and goes in series. Swapping them gives a reading that is not the quantity asked for.

**Recap the learner should be able to say:**

- Current is charge flow per second, voltage is energy per unit charge, and resistance is the opposition that links them: V = IR.
- Series: same current, voltages add, resistances add. Parallel: same voltage, currents add, total resistance is less than the smallest branch.
- Power is P = VI; a higher voltage can deliver the same power at a lower current, which reduces heating losses in transmission.

## 8. Teacher run-of-show

### Before anyone touches the model

1. Build one cell and one bulb with an ammeter, then add a second identical bulb in series with the meter still in place. Ask for a prediction before closing the switch.
1. Move the switch to the other side of the loop and ask whether the brightness changes.

### Questions to ask while it runs

- Is the current the same before and after the first bulb? What is used up — charge or energy?
- Two cells and two bulbs is as bright as one cell and one bulb. What does that tell you about what brightness depends on?

### One guided activity

Groups are given the model and a table to complete: cells, bulbs, current, brightness. They must predict each row before pressing the button and mark whether the prediction held.

### With no device

A circuit diagram to annotate with ammeter readings, and a six-row prediction table for cells and bulbs.

### What to look for

The belief is visible in the reasoning: 'the cell gives a fixed current' predicts 'same'. Learners who fix the model but still explain with 'more power' need the V = IR substitution made explicit.

### Where to go next

- Parallel circuits and the resistance rule.
- Electrical power and the heating effect.
