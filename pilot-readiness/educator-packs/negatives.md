## 1. What the learner is meant to be able to do

Compare two numbers below zero, and say which way the answer goes when you move along the line.

**The prediction the learner must commit to before touching the model:** The marker is at −5. Is −5 greater than −2, less than −2, or the same?

- Greater — 5 is bigger than 2
- **Less — it is further left** *(the answer the model shows)*
- The same — the sign does not change the size

**What the reveal says once a choice is made:** Less. On the left of zero the order runs the other way: −5 is further from zero than −2 but further LEFT, and further left is smaller. The digits say 5 is bigger than 2; the line says the number is lower. That is exactly why the model prints both neighbours of the marker.

**What the learner does with it afterwards:** Move the marker twice more and say which way the number went before you read the sentence.

**Starting point the lesson assumes:** An underground car park already uses this idea. You are parked on level −2 and you drive up four floors. You do not arrive at 5: you arrive at 3, because you started below street level and passing zero changes nothing about the counting. Every rule about negatives is that one picture — a number line where zero is not a wall you stop at, but a line you can walk straight past.

## 3. Design decision

Order is read off a line, not off digits. The learner moves a marker in ones on a line that runs either side of zero, and the sentence prints the two neighbours either side of it, so '−5 is less than −2' is a position rather than a rule to memorise. Distance from zero and side of zero are reported separately, because 'further from zero feels bigger' is exactly the confusion (Young & Booth 2015).


### Misconceptions the lesson is built to refute

- **further-is-bigger** — −5 is greater than −2, because 5 is greater than 2. _(mrbarton-negative-numbers-probes)_
- **opposite-sign-placement** — Negative numbers belong on the right of zero, or positives on the left. _(widodo-2018-number-line-misconceptions)_
- **distance-is-value** — The number furthest from zero is the greatest. _(young-booth-2015-negative-magnitude)_

## 4. The model: assumptions and limits

**Intended level:** 11–14 (negative numbers, decimals to one place, place value in tens)

**Assumptions (scientific/mathematical):**

- The line is linear and uniform: equal steps are equal distances.
- One move is the concept's unit — one, one tenth, or ten — and cannot be varied within a concept.
- Decimals are shown to one decimal place only.
- Place value is represented on a 0–1000 line stepping in tens.
- Zero is a point on the line, not a direction.

**What it cannot show:**

- No hundredths, so decimal place-value beyond tenths is not shown.
- No negative fractions or negative decimals.
- The place-value line stops at 1000 and has only a tens step; hundreds and units are not separately manipulable.
- The line cannot show density (that infinitely many numbers lie between two points).

**Misconceptions it addresses:**

- −5 is greater than −2 because 5 is greater than 2.
- The number furthest from zero is the greatest.
- 0.45 is greater than 0.5 because 45 is greater than 5.
- More digits means a larger number.

**Misconceptions it might accidentally introduce — please check these first:**

- That decimals have only one decimal place.
- That a place-value number is always smaller than 1000.
- That distance from zero and value are the same thing on the right-hand side, which is true there and may be over-generalised to the left.

## 6. The teaching sequence

_This plan has no authored sections; the page groups its idea paragraphs. Treat the sequence as unreviewed structure._

A negative number is an amount below zero. −4 sits the same distance from zero as 4 does, on the other side, which is why the sign is the only thing separating them: the distance of −4 from zero is 4, exactly as it is for 4. The sign says direction, not size.

Adding and subtracting are moves along the line. Adding a negative moves left: 6 + (−4) = 2. Subtracting a negative needs a second look, because it takes away a leftward move, which leaves you further right: 5 − (−3) = 5 + 3 = 8. You do not have to memorise that. 5 − (−3) asks "what number added to −3 gives 5?", and only 8 does.

Multiplying is repeated addition, so signs follow the moves. (−3) × 4 is four jumps of three to the left: −12. (−3) × (−4) reverses direction twice — taking away four debts of 3 leaves you 12 better off — so the answer is positive. One negative gives a negative answer; two negatives give a positive one.

Comparing catches people out because the line runs the other way from the numbers: −2 is greater than −5, even though 5 is greater than 2. Rightwards is larger. That single fact is also why an inequality flips sign the moment you multiply or divide by a negative.

## 7. Questions to ask and traps to expect

- **Turning 5 − (−3) into 5 − 3 = 2.** The minus in front acts on a value that is already negative; it does not cancel the subtraction. On the line, subtracting a leftward move of three has to take you further right, and 5 + 3 = 8 is the only value for which 8 + (−3) comes back to 5.
- **Saying −8 is bigger than −3 because 8 is bigger than 3.** Distance from zero is not size. −3 sits three steps to the RIGHT of −8, and rightwards is larger, so −3 > −8. Learners who reorder numbers this way later read inequalities backwards, which is why this one is worth fixing now.
- **Working out (−2) × (−3) as −6.** Both signs reverse the direction, so the result points back to the positive side. Test it against the rule you already trust: (−2) × 3 = −6, so (−2) × (−3) must be the opposite of that, which is 6.

## 8. Teacher run-of-show

### Before anyone touches the model

1. Draw a vertical number line as a lift shaft in an underground car park: ground is 0, each floor below is negative. Ask which floor is lower, −2 or −5.
1. Mark −3 and ask for the number one step right and one step left, so the direction is read off the diagram rather than the digits.

### Questions to ask while it runs

- Which is colder, −2 °C or −5 °C, and what does that tell you about which number is smaller?
- How far is −5 from zero, and is that distance the same as its value?

### One guided activity

Give pairs a shuffled set of cards from −10 to +10 and one number line. They order the cards, then check each neighbouring pair against the line.

### With no device

A −10 to +10 line to draw on, ten integers to place, and three comparison statements to complete with < or >.

### What to look for

Look for learners comparing by distance from zero rather than by position. A correct answer with the reason 'because 5 is bigger' is not yet the idea — ask which is further left.

### Where to go next

- Adding and subtracting negatives.
- Coordinates in four quadrants.
