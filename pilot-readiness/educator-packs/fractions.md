## The question this review answers

Not "is the interface pleasant". **Would you use this to teach the concept, and what would you change before using it with your pupils?** Correct the subject accuracy first; then the level, the wording, and the model's simplifications.

## 1. What the learner is meant to be able to do

Read a fraction as a position on a number line, and say why 1/8 is smaller than 1/4 although 8 is bigger.

**The prediction the learner must commit to before touching the model:** Which is the larger number: 1/8 or 1/4?

- 1/8 — 8 is bigger than 4
- **1/4 — the pieces are bigger** *(the answer the model shows)*
- They are the same

**What the reveal says once a choice is made:** 1/4 is larger. Cutting the same whole into more pieces makes each piece smaller, so one of eight is nearer zero than one of four. On the line, 1/8 sits to the LEFT of 1/4 — the position is the fact, and the digits alone say the opposite.

**What the learner does with it afterwards:** Change the cut and predict which way the marker will move before you press.

**Starting point the lesson assumes:** Two chocolate bars, identical. One is broken into four pieces, the other into eight, and you are handed one piece of each. The eight-piece bar gave you the SMALLER piece even though 8 is the bigger number — because the number underneath the fraction is not how much you get, it is how many pieces the bar was cut into. Every fraction question starts by asking which of those two things the number is doing.

## 3. Design decision

A fraction is taught as a POSITION before it is practised as a calculation. The learner cuts one whole into equal pieces, sees each piece shrink as the denominator rises, and then reads the same fraction as a marker on a 0–1 line against a 1/2 benchmark. The bar stays because it explains piece size; the line leads because accuracy at estimating magnitude on a line predicted fewer whole-number ordering errors, while part-whole shading did not (Bailey et al. 2016). Comparison is taught by renaming both fractions in the same piece size, and the model says out loud when comparing the bottoms would happen to give the right answer — because that coincidence is what keeps the wrong rule alive.


### Misconceptions the lesson is built to refute

- **bigger-bottom-bigger-piece** — 1/8 is bigger than 1/2 because 8 is bigger than 2. _(ejceel-2024-fractions-basic7)_
- **compare-by-denominator** — To compare two fractions, compare the bottom numbers. _(newstead-murray-1998-fractions)_
- **whole-number-ordering** — Fractions can be ordered by treating the numerator and denominator as separate whole numbers. _(pmc-2016-fraction-ordering)_

## 4. The model: assumptions and limits

**Intended level:** 9–14 (fractions as numbers; comparison and equivalence)

**Assumptions (scientific/mathematical):**

- The 0–1 interval is one whole, and the bar and the line represent the same whole.
- The whole is cut into equal pieces, and the numerator counts them.
- Only values from 0 to 1 are shown: proper fractions, including 1.
- Denominators run from 2 to 12, and the 1/2 landmark is the comparison benchmark.
- The comparison shown (3/4 against 5/8) is fixed, so the learner explores one fraction and reads one worked comparison.

**What it cannot show:**

- No fractions greater than one, mixed numbers or negative fractions.
- No fraction density: the line does not show that infinitely many fractions lie between two points.
- No equivalence family interaction beyond the fixed comparison, so renaming is explained rather than explored.
- One decimal place is not shown on this line, so fraction–decimal equivalence is not covered.

**Misconceptions it addresses:**

- 1/8 is bigger than 1/4 because 8 is bigger than 4.
- Fractions are compared by their bottom numbers.
- A learner who can shade a part of a shape can therefore order fractions.

**Misconceptions it might accidentally introduce — please check these first:**

- That fractions are always less than one, because the line stops at 1.
- That the marker's position is an exact measurement rather than a diagram of a value.
- That every fraction diagram is pre-partitioned into equal pieces, which is not how a learner meets a fraction in a problem.
- That a fraction's SIZE is fixed regardless of the whole it is taken from: 3/5 of a smaller whole can be a shorter piece than 1/5 of a larger one, and this model's whole never changes.

## 6. The teaching sequence

_This plan has no authored sections; the page groups its idea paragraphs. Treat the sequence as unreviewed structure._

A fraction counts equal pieces of one whole. In 3/4 the bottom number, 4, says the whole was cut into four equal pieces; the top number, 3, says how many of them you have. Say it out loud that way — "three pieces, each a quarter" — and the notation stops being two numbers stacked up.

The bottom number is a SIZE, not a quantity. Cutting a bar into more pieces makes each piece smaller, so a bigger denominator means a smaller slice: 1/8 < 1/4. This is the single most common wrong answer in the whole of fractions, and it is not carelessness — it is reading the denominator as an amount, which is exactly what the top number is for.

Equivalent fractions say the same amount in different pieces. 1/2 = 2/4 = 3/6 because multiplying the top and the bottom by the same number doubles the count AND halves the slice size, so the amount of chocolate is unchanged. That is why you may scale a fraction up or down as long as you scale both parts: you are renaming the pieces, not changing the amount.

To compare fractions with different bottoms, make the pieces the same size — a common denominator. 2/3 and 3/4 become 8/12 and 9/12, and now the tops can be compared directly. The other reliable method needs no arithmetic: compare each fraction with 1/2. A fraction whose top is less than half its bottom (3/8) is below 1/2, and one whose top is more than half its bottom (5/8) is above it, so 3/8 < 5/8 and you never had to compute anything.

A fraction is ONE number, not a division you have not finished. 3/4 sits at a definite place on the number line, between 0 and 1, closer to 1 than to 0 — and once you can see it there, adding, comparing and scaling it are operations on a number rather than rules about two integers that happen to be written on top of each other.

Decimals and percentages are the same idea in other notation: 3/4 = 0.75 = 75%. Nothing about the quantity changes when the notation does, and a learner who knows where 3/4 sits on the line already knows most of what the next two concepts teach.

## 7. Questions to ask and traps to expect

- **"1/8 is bigger than 1/4 because 8 is bigger than 4."** The bottom number counts the pieces, so a bigger bottom means each piece is smaller. Cut the same bar into eight and every piece is half the size of the quarter you had before. Compare the slices, not the numbers.
- **"2/3 is bigger than 3/4 because 2 and 3 are bigger than 3 and 4 in the other one."** Comparing tops alone only works when the bottoms match. Here the pieces are different sizes, so 2 of one size and 3 of another cannot be counted against each other until the sizes are made equal: 8/12 against 9/12.
- **"3/4 + 1/4 = 4/8."** Adding the bottoms together changes the size of every piece in the answer, so the addition is no longer about the same wholes. Quarters and quarters are the same size, so the tops simply add: 3/4 + 1/4 = 1. (Different bottoms are next concept, and they need a common denominator first.)
- **"3/5 is bigger than 1/5, so 3/5 of anything is bigger than 1/5 of anything."** That holds only while both fractions are taken from the SAME whole. 3/5 of a short bar can be a shorter piece than 1/5 of a long one — cut two bars of different lengths and check. A fraction describes a share of a particular whole, so the first question is always what the whole is; a comparison that never names its whole is not yet a comparison.

## 8. Teacher run-of-show

### Before anyone touches the model

1. Fold one strip of paper into quarters and an identical strip into eighths. Put them one above the other on the desk so the shorter piece is visible, not asserted.
1. Mark both fractions on a single number line from 0 to 1 and ask which is nearer 1/2.

### Questions to ask while it runs

- The bottom number got bigger — what happened to the piece, and why?
- Which is nearer one half: 3/8 or 3/4? How can you tell from the line without a calculation?

### One guided activity

Pairs cut a strip into equal pieces, label each cut as a fraction of the whole, then place a counter at 3/4 and 5/8 and decide which is further right.

### With no device

Two strips to fold (quarters and eighths), a 0–1 line to mark, and four comparisons to decide with the line only.

### What to look for

The idea is present when the learner points at the line rather than reasoning from the digits. 'The bigger bottom means a bigger fraction' is the misconception; re-teach with the fold, not with more shading.

### Where to go next

- Equivalent fractions.
- Adding fractions with the same denominator.
