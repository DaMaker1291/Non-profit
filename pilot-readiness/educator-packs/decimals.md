## The question this review answers

Not "is the interface pleasant". **Would you use this to teach the concept, and what would you change before using it with your pupils?** Correct the subject accuracy first; then the level, the wording, and the model's simplifications.

## 1. What the learner is meant to be able to do

Read a decimal by its place value, and place two decimals in order without being misled by the number of digits.

**The prediction the learner must commit to before touching the model:** Which is larger: 0.45 or 0.5?

- 0.45 — 45 is bigger than 5
- **0.5 — five tenths is more than four tenths** *(the answer the model shows)*
- They are the same

**What the reveal says once a choice is made:** 0.5 is larger: it is five tenths, while 0.45 is four tenths and five hundredths. More digits does not mean a bigger number — the place each digit sits in decides its value, which is what the line shows when you step in tenths.

**What the learner does with it afterwards:** Place three decimals on the line in your head and put them in order before you check with the marker.

**Starting point the lesson assumes:** Two prices on a shelf: £0.50 and £0.45. The second one has MORE digits, and the first is worth more — 50p beats 45p. That is the whole difficulty of decimals in one comparison: the digits are not the amount, their position is. A decimal continues the place-value system whole numbers already use, past a point and into tenths, hundredths and thousandths, so the column a digit sits in decides what it counts.

## 3. Design decision

Place value is shown by MOVING in tenths, not by comparing digit strings. The model's step is one tenth, the ticks are counted in tenths, and the sentence says how many tenths the marker is from zero — which is the fact 'longer is larger' ignores (Steinle & Stacey 1998).


### Misconceptions the lesson is built to refute

- **string-length** — 4.63 is bigger than 4.8 because it has more digits. _(steinle-stacey-1998-decimals)_
- **decimal-point-ignored** — 0.65 is the whole number 65. _(widodo-2018-number-line-misconceptions)_
- **more-digits-is-bigger** — 0.45 is greater than 0.5, because 45 is greater than 5. _(mrbarton-negative-numbers-probes)_

## 4. The model: assumptions and limits

**Intended level:** 11–14 (negative numbers, decimals to one place, place value in tens)

**Assumptions (scientific/mathematical):**

- The line is linear and uniform: equal steps are equal distances.
- One move is the concept's unit — one, one tenth, or ten — and cannot be varied within a concept.
- Decimals are shown to one decimal place only.
- Place value is represented on a 0–1000 line stepping in tens.
- Zero is a point on the line, not a direction.

**What it cannot show:**

- No hundredths, so decimal place-value beyond tenths is not shown.
- No negative fractions or negative decimals.
- The place-value line stops at 1000 and has only a tens step; hundreds and units are not separately manipulable.
- The line cannot show density (that infinitely many numbers lie between two points).

**Misconceptions it addresses:**

- −5 is greater than −2 because 5 is greater than 2.
- The number furthest from zero is the greatest.
- 0.45 is greater than 0.5 because 45 is greater than 5.
- More digits means a larger number.

**Misconceptions it might accidentally introduce — please check these first:**

- That decimals have only one decimal place.
- That a place-value number is always smaller than 1000.
- That distance from zero and value are the same thing on the right-hand side, which is true there and may be over-generalised to the left.

## 6. The teaching sequence

_This plan has no authored sections; the page groups its idea paragraphs. Treat the sequence as unreviewed structure._

Read the point as the word "and": 0.45 is 4 tenths and 5 hundredths, which is 45/100. Everything to the right of the point is a fraction whose denominator is a power of ten, and each step further right divides the value by ten again — tenths, then hundredths, then thousandths. A digit's position is doing exactly the work it does in 452, where the 4 means 400, except now it continues into amounts smaller than one.

Comparing decimals is decided column by column, not by how long the number is. Write them with the same number of places — 0.5 becomes 0.50 — and compare from the left: the tenths decide it, and only if they tie do you look at the hundredths. That is why 0.09 is smaller than 0.1 despite 9 being bigger than 1: 0.09 has no tenths at all, and 0.1 has one.

To add or subtract, put the points in a line — one number under the other with the points in the same column — fill any gaps with zeros, and add the columns exactly as you would whole numbers. 0.3 + 0.4 is three tenths plus four tenths: seven tenths, 0.7. The point is not written in because it was moved; it stays in the column it started in, which is why lining it up is the whole method.

Multiplying needs a different routine, because the columns no longer have to match. Multiply the digits as if the points were not there, then count the decimal places in BOTH factors and put that many back into the answer: 3 × 2 = 6, and 0.3 and 0.2 have one place each, so the answer needs two — 0.06. That count is not a trick; tenths times tenths really is hundredths, which is the same fact as 1/10 × 1/10 = 1/100.

A decimal is one quantity written in another notation, so converting costs nothing: 0.45 = 45/100 = 45%. The same number can be a price, a length, a probability or a percentage, and none of those changes its size. What does change is the precision a written decimal claims — 2.4 said to one decimal place means "between 2.35 and 2.45" — which is why rounding is a decision about which column matters rather than a tidy-up.

Multiplying or dividing by ten shifts the digits, not the quantity: 0.45 × 10 = 4.5 and 0.45 ÷ 10 = 0.045, with the point moving one place each time because every digit has moved one column. That is the one legitimate use of "move the point", and saying the operation out loud — "divide by ten" — keeps it attached to something true.

## 7. Questions to ask and traps to expect

- **"0.45 is bigger than 0.5, because 45 is bigger than 5."** Comparing the digits as if the point were not there ignores the column each one is in. Give both numbers the same number of places — 0.50 against 0.45 — and the tenths decide: five tenths beat four tenths. A longer decimal is not a bigger amount; 0.09 is smaller than 0.1.
- **"0.3 × 0.2 = 0.6."** That is 0.3 × 2 — the point moved one place when the two factors had two decimal places between them. 3 × 2 = 6, so two places back gives 0.06. The check that catches it: multiplying two numbers smaller than 1 must give an answer smaller than either of them, and 0.6 is bigger than 0.3.
- **"0.3 + 0.4 = 7, because 3 + 4 = 7."** Dropping the points multiplied both numbers by ten, so the answer is ten times too big. Three tenths plus four tenths is seven tenths, 0.7. Write the numbers one under the other with the points in a column: the digits you are adding are then the ones in the same place value.

## 8. Teacher run-of-show

### Before anyone touches the model

1. Write 0.45 and 0.5 side by side and read both aloud as fractions of a hundred: 45 hundredths against 50 hundredths.
1. Then show them on one line to tenths and ask why 0.45 is not at a tick.

### Questions to ask while it runs

- Which digit tells you the most about the size: the first one after the point or the last?
- 0.45 has more digits than 0.5. Why is that not evidence that it is bigger?

### One guided activity

Pairs order a set of decimal cards by placing them on a 0–2 line first, then checking the order by reading each aloud as hundredths.

### With no device

A 0–2 line marked in tenths, six decimals to place, and a table to write each as 'tenths and hundredths'.

### What to look for

A learner who orders correctly but cannot read 0.45 as 45 hundredths has a rule, not a concept. Ask them to say the number aloud in words.

### Where to go next

- Multiplying and dividing decimals by ten.
- Rounding to one and two decimal places.
