## The question this review answers

Not "is the interface pleasant". **Would you use this to teach the concept, and what would you change before using it with your pupils?** Correct the subject accuracy first; then the level, the wording, and the model's simplifications.

## 1. What the learner is meant to be able to do

Say what each digit in a number is worth, and show what moving one place does to its value.

**The prediction the learner must commit to before touching the model:** The line's ticks are tens and the marker is at 340. How many moves from zero is that?

- **34 moves** *(the answer the model shows)*
- 340 moves
- 3.4 moves

**What the reveal says once a choice is made:** 34 moves: one move is ten, so 340 is thirty-four tens. The digit 3 is worth 300 — thirty moves — and the 4 adds four more. Counting the ticks rather than the digits is the whole point: '340 moves' counts ones on a line that has none.

**What the learner does with it afterwards:** Pick any number on the line and say its value in tens before you read the sentence under it.

**Starting point the lesson assumes:** A bus goes past with the number 45 on the front. A price tag reads £45. Same two digits, and one of them is nine times bigger than the other number's twin. The digits did not change; their POSITION did. That is the whole idea, and it is why 405 and 45 are not the same amount of anything.

## 3. Design decision

A place is a NUMBER OF MOVES, so the line's step is the place being taught: on a 0–1000 line one move is ten, and 340 is 34 moves from zero. The WWC guide's warning is built in — the whole visible line is labelled as the extent of the number system rather than implicitly one unit, and the tick count is named in tens so a learner cannot read the line as 0 to 34.


### Misconceptions the lesson is built to refute

- **string-length** — More digits means a larger number, whatever the place. _(steinle-stacey-1998-decimals)_
- **decimal-point-ignored** — The digits after a point are separate whole numbers rather than places. _(widodo-2018-number-line-misconceptions)_
- **whole-line-as-unit** — A fraction is located as a proportion of the whole visible line rather than of the 0–1 interval. _(wwc-2010-fractions-guide)_

## 4. The model: assumptions and limits

**Intended level:** 11–14 (negative numbers, decimals to one place, place value in tens)

**Assumptions (scientific/mathematical):**

- The line is linear and uniform: equal steps are equal distances.
- One move is the concept's unit — one, one tenth, or ten — and cannot be varied within a concept.
- Decimals are shown to one decimal place only.
- Place value is represented on a 0–1000 line stepping in tens.
- Zero is a point on the line, not a direction.

**What it cannot show:**

- No hundredths, so decimal place-value beyond tenths is not shown.
- No negative fractions or negative decimals.
- The place-value line stops at 1000 and has only a tens step; hundreds and units are not separately manipulable.
- The line cannot show density (that infinitely many numbers lie between two points).

**Misconceptions it addresses:**

- −5 is greater than −2 because 5 is greater than 2.
- The number furthest from zero is the greatest.
- 0.45 is greater than 0.5 because 45 is greater than 5.
- More digits means a larger number.

**Misconceptions it might accidentally introduce — please check these first:**

- That decimals have only one decimal place.
- That a place-value number is always smaller than 1000.
- That distance from zero and value are the same thing on the right-hand side, which is true there and may be over-generalised to the left.

## 6. The teaching sequence

_This plan has no authored sections; the page groups its idea paragraphs. Treat the sequence as unreviewed structure._

Every column is ten times the column on its right. So 452 is not "four, five, two": it is 4 hundreds + 5 tens + 2 ones = 400 + 50 + 2. The digit 4 is worth 400 here, 40 in 45, and 4 in 4 — the same symbol, three different amounts.

Zero is a placeholder, not decoration. 405 has no tens, and the zero is what holds the hundreds column in its place. Drop it and you have written 45, a different number. This is also why you cannot "cancel" a zero off the end of a number unless you are dividing by ten.

Multiplying by 10 moves every digit one column LEFT, because each digit becomes ten times bigger: 35 × 10 = 350. Dividing by 10 moves them right, which is why 350 ÷ 10 = 35. The decimal point never moves — the digits do, and the point stays where it is to mark the ones column.

Decimals continue the same pattern to the right. The column after the point is tenths, then hundredths: 0.1 is one tenth, 0.45 is four tenths and five hundredths. Nothing new starts at the point; the tens pattern simply keeps halving in the other direction.

Comparing numbers means comparing the leftmost column where they differ, not counting digits. 0.45 and 0.5 look like 45 against 5, but line up the columns: 0.45 is 45 hundredths and 0.5 is 50 hundredths, so 0.5 is larger. The same rule settles 4,099 against 4,100.

This is the machinery under every written method you will meet. Column addition lines up the columns so ones meet ones; long multiplication splits a number into its place-value parts; standard form writes a number as a digit string times a power of ten. A learner who cannot say what a digit is worth cannot check any of them.

## 7. Questions to ask and traps to expect

- **Saying the 4 in 452 is worth 4.** The value comes from the column, not the symbol. That 4 sits in the hundreds column, so it contributes 400; the number would be 52 without it. Ask "which column is it in?" before "what is the digit?".
- **Deciding 0.45 is bigger than 0.5 because 45 is bigger than 5.** They are not in the same column. 0.45 is 45 hundredths and 0.5 is 5 tenths, which is 50 hundredths — so 0.5 is bigger. Compare by place, and pad with zeros if it helps: 0.45 against 0.50.
- **Multiplying by 10 by writing a zero on the end.** That happens to work for whole numbers and quietly fails for decimals: 3.5 × 10 is 35, not 3.50. The digits move one column left; writing a zero does not move anything, it just adds a placeholder.

**Recap the learner should be able to say:**

- Each column is ten times the one on its right, so 452 = 400 + 50 + 2.
- Zero holds a column open; 405 is not 45.
- Multiplying by 10 moves the digits one column left; the decimal point stays put.

## 8. Teacher run-of-show

### Before anyone touches the model

1. Draw a line on the board marked 0, 100, 200 … 1000 and put a marker at 340. Ask how many jumps of ten that is before anyone touches a device.
1. Then move the marker one tick and ask what has changed about the number: the tens digit, not the hundreds.

### Questions to ask while it runs

- Which digit changed when we moved one tick, and by how much is the number now different?
- If one move is ten, what would one move have to be to show the 4 changing?

### One guided activity

Pairs: one learner names a number on the line, the other says how many tens it is from zero and which digit the move changed. Swap after five numbers.

### With no device

A blank 0–1000 number line in tens, with six markers to place and a column for 'value of the 3' and 'value of the 4'.

### What to look for

A learner who has the idea counts ticks in tens and says which digit moved. A learner who says '340 moves' is still counting ones on a line whose unit is ten — the misconception this model exists for, and it needs the unit named again, not more practice.

### Where to go next

- Multiplying and dividing by ten and a hundred.
- Reading and writing large numbers from their digits.
