## maths: Data representation (`data-charts`)

**Why this lesson:** A table part, a pie-angle calculation and a representation-choice explanation.

**Curriculum:** GCSE Mathematics, statistics: bar charts, line graphs, pie charts and scatter graphs; choosing, constructing and criticising a representation · Key Stage 3 to GCSE Foundation

**Prerequisites:** averages

**Version:** 1.1.0 · **Declared status:** draft · **Reviews:** 0 · **Learner testing:** 0

### Objectives

- **Choose a chart from the type of data and the question being asked, and explain why the alternatives are weaker.**  
  Evidence: For unordered categories the learner chooses a bar chart, for change over time a line graph, for shares of one whole a pie chart, and for two measured variables a scatter graph, giving a reason for each choice rather than naming a favourite.
- **Read a chart honestly by finding its total, its scale and its baseline, and explain how a truncated axis changes the comparison the picture makes.**  
  Evidence: Given bars of 32 and 36 on axes starting at 0 and at 30, the learner says the true rise is 4 (12.5%) while the truncated chart draws heights of 2 and 6, and that the labels are true even when the visual ratio is not.
- **Construct a bar chart and a pie chart from a frequency table, calculate each pie angle, and state what the chart does not show.**  
  Evidence: For 60 learners travelling by walk, bus, car and cycle the learner obtains 90°, 144°, 72° and 54°, checks the angles sum to 360°, and explains that the pie shows each share but not the total of 60 or the count in each category.

### Explanation

#### The question decides the chart

A bar chart compares counts or amounts across categories. Its bars are separate because the categories are separate: football, netball and swimming have no order, so the gap between the bars is information. A line graph is different. It is used when the horizontal axis is an ordered sequence, usually time, because the segment between two points asserts that the values in between are meaningful. Drawing a line through favourite colours joins points that have no in-between, so the trend it appears to show was invented by the chart.
A pie chart is for parts of one whole: every category is a share of the same total, and the angles sum to 360°. A scatter graph is for two measured variables on the same individual, such as height and shoe size, and it can show a relationship without either variable being a category. Choosing a chart is therefore part of answering the question. A pie with twelve thin slices is unreadable; a bar chart with forty categories is a wall; a line through unordered categories is a false claim about continuity. The chart should make the intended comparison easy, not decorate the table.

#### A scale is part of the argument

In a bar chart the length of the bar encodes the value, so the baseline is part of the measurement. If the axis starts at 0, a value of 36 is 90% of a full-scale 40 and a value of 32 is 80%, which is the ratio the numbers actually have. If the axis starts at 30, the same two values become bars of 6 and 2, and the second looks three times the first. The labels are still correct; the lengths are not proportional to the values, so the visual comparison is false. A truncated bar chart is not lying about the numbers, it is lying with the lengths.
A line graph encodes value by position rather than length, so a truncated vertical axis exaggerates a change without changing the shape of the line. That can be legitimate when the interesting variation is small, provided the axis is labelled and the reader is told, but it is misleading when the picture is presented as the whole story. Equal intervals matter on both axes: a gap of 10 must be the same distance wherever it appears, or the slope and the spacing stop meaning anything. Axes need names and units, because a number without a unit cannot be checked.

#### Pie charts encode shares, not counts

A pie chart turns each category's share of the total into an angle. The whole circle is 360°, so a category with a quarter of the total gets a quarter of 360°, which is 90°. For 60 learners travelling by walk, bus, car and cycle in the counts 15, 24, 12 and 9, the shares are 25%, 40%, 20% and 15%, and the angles are 90°, 144°, 72° and 54°. The four angles must add to 360°; if they do not, either a count or a division is wrong. The largest sector is the bus at 144°, which is 40% of the learners.
The angle is a share, so it cannot tell you a count without the total. Half of 40 learners is 20, while a quarter of 200 is 50: the second slice is smaller and the number of learners is larger. Across two pie charts with different totals, a bigger angle does not mean more items; within one chart, it does mean a larger share. A pie also hides the total itself, because the circle is always 100% however many people were asked. That is why a report should print the total and the counts beside the chart rather than leaving the reader to infer them.

#### Reading critically: total, scale, missing

Three questions catch most misleading charts. What is the total? A percentage without a base is unreadable, and two charts with different totals cannot be compared by angle. What does each axis do? Check where the vertical axis starts, whether the intervals are equal, and whether both axes are labelled with units. What is missing? Non-responses, merged categories, a change in what was counted, or a source that stopped at a convenient year can matter more than anything drawn. A chart can be accurate in every number and still mislead by what it leaves out.
The defence is arithmetic rather than suspicion. Read the values, recompute a share or a difference, and compare it with what the picture appears to claim. In a scatter graph, a pattern is not a cause: two variables can rise together because a third factor drives both, and the genome's next concept, correlation, is where that distinction is taught. When you make a chart, state the total, the scale and the units, and start bars at zero, so a reader can check the argument instead of trusting the shape.

### Representations

**maths.expression** — A frequency table becomes a bar chart by using each count as the height of its bar. The bars start at zero so their lengths stay proportional to the counts.

```text
football 12 · netball 8 · swimming 5 · athletics 3 · total 28 · scale 0 to 12, step 2
```

Caption: The tallest bar is football at 12 and the shortest is athletics at 3, a ratio of four to one that the zero baseline preserves. If the axis started at 10, those same bars would be 2 and -7 units, which is impossible, or 2 and 0, which reverses the order.

**maths.expression** — A pie chart turns each category's share into an angle. The whole circle is the total, so every angle is share × 360° and the angles must add to 360°.

```text
travel to school, 60 learners: walk 15, bus 24, car 12, cycle 9   ·   angles 90°, 144°, 72°, 54°   ·   sum 360°   ·   bus = 40%
```

Caption: The bus sector is the largest at 144°, which is 40% of the 60 learners, or 24 people. The pie shows the share clearly and the total not at all: the same angles would describe 6 learners or 600.

**maths.expression** — The same two values can look almost equal or three times apart depending on where the bar axis starts. The numbers do not change; the lengths do.

```text
cycling 32 → 36   ·   true change +4 = 12.5%   ·   axis 0–40: heights 80% and 90%   ·   axis 30–40: heights 2 and 6 = 3×
```

Caption: On a zero baseline the rise is one tenth of a full bar and looks small, which is honest. Starting at 30 draws the same rise as a bar three times as tall, which is the picture the headline 'tripled' came from.

### Worked example

**A table records favourite sports: football 12, netball 8, swimming 5 and athletics 3. Build a bar chart and state its scale and total.**

1. **Add the counts to find the total: 12 + 8 + 5 + 3 = 28 learners were asked.** — The total is the whole the bars are parts of. Without it, a reader cannot turn a count into a share or check that every learner is represented once.
2. **Choose a bar chart, because the categories have no natural order and the question compares their counts.** — A line would imply that football, netball and swimming sit on an ordered scale, which they do not. A pie would show shares, but the comparison asked for is between counts.
3. **Set the vertical axis from 0 to 12 with steps of 2, label it 'number of learners', and draw each bar to its count.** — A zero baseline keeps bar length proportional to the count. A step of 2 fits the largest value of 12 without compressing the smaller bars into illegibility.
4. **Check the chart against the table: the football bar is 12, the athletics bar is 3, and the four bars account for all 28 learners.** — The chart has to preserve both the individual values and the total. A bar drawn to the wrong height is a false number even when the table beside it is right.

Answer: A bar chart with a 0–12 axis, bars of 12, 8, 5 and 3, labelled in learners; total 28.

**A survey of 60 learners records how they travel: walk 15, bus 24, car 12, cycle 9. Draw a pie chart, calculating each angle.**

1. **Check the total: 15 + 24 + 12 + 9 = 60.** — Every share is a fraction of this total, and a pie is only valid when the categories account for the whole group exactly once.
2. **Turn each count into a share and then an angle: 15/60 × 360° = 90°, 24/60 × 360° = 144°, 12/60 × 360° = 72°, 9/60 × 360° = 54°.** — The angle is the share of the circle, so the same fraction that gives the proportion gives the angle. Multiplying by 360° converts a fraction of the whole into degrees.
3. **Check the angles sum to 360°: 90 + 144 + 72 + 54 = 360.** — The four sectors must fill the circle exactly. A sum above or below 360° means a count, a total or a division is wrong, and the check catches it before the chart is drawn.
4. **The bus sector is 144°, the largest; it is 40% of the learners, which is 24 people. The pie shows the share but not the 60 or the 24 unless they are printed beside it.** — A reader can see that bus is the largest share without knowing how many learners were asked. The count needs the total, which is why the total belongs in the title or the key.

Answer: Angles 90°, 144°, 72° and 54°, summing to 360°; bus is the largest share at 40% (24 learners).

**A chart shows cycling rising from 32 to 36 with a vertical axis starting at 30. Explain what the picture exaggerates and what the true change is.**

1. **Find the true change: 36 - 32 = 4, and 4 ÷ 32 = 0.125, so the rise is 12.5%.** — The percentage compares the change with the starting value. It is the honest size of the change, independent of how the axis is drawn.
2. **Read the bar heights the axis actually draws: with a baseline of 30, the bars are 36 - 30 = 6 and 32 - 30 = 2.** — Bar length encodes value above the baseline. The labels 32 and 36 are true, but the lengths a reader compares are 2 and 6.
3. **Compare the visual ratio with the true ratio: 6 ÷ 2 = 3, while 36 ÷ 32 = 1.125. The chart makes a 12.5% rise look like a tripling.** — The reader compares lengths, not printed labels. A truncated axis turns a small change into a large-looking one, which is why the headline and the picture agree with each other and not with the data.
4. **The honest chart starts at 0, where the bars are 32 and 36 and the true rise of 4 (12.5%) looks small; the 30-based chart's heights of 2 and 6 are what turn that rise into a three-times picture, so a truncated axis must be disclosed and the values read.** — A zero baseline preserves the ratio. Truncation can be legitimate, but only when it is disclosed, because the picture no longer carries the comparison on its own.

Answer: The true rise is 4, or 12.5%; the 30-based axis draws bars of 2 and 6, a three-times picture, so the chart exaggerates a small change.

### Misconceptions

- **Wrong belief:** A pie chart shows the counts, so a bigger slice always means more items, even when comparing two different charts.
  **Why it fails:** A pie angle is a share of that chart's total, not a count. Half of 40 learners is 20, while a quarter of 200 is 50: the second slice is smaller and represents more learners. The circle is always 100%, so the total is invisible unless it is printed.
  **Correction:** Use angles to compare shares within one chart, and counts to compare across charts. Print the total and the category counts beside the pie, and recompute a count from the share only when the total is known.
- **Wrong belief:** Any data can be shown on a line graph, because joining the points shows the trend.
  **Why it fails:** A line asserts that the horizontal axis is ordered and that values between the points are meaningful. Favourite colours, makes of car and types of pet have no in-between, so a line through them invents a sequence and a trend that the data never had.
  **Correction:** Use a line only when the horizontal axis is an ordered sequence, usually time. For unordered categories use a bar chart, which keeps the categories separate and does not imply a value between them.
- **Wrong belief:** Starting a bar chart's axis at 30 instead of 0 is acceptable, because the bars are still labelled with the true values.
  **Why it fails:** A bar's length is the value above the baseline, and that length is what a reader compares. Starting at 30 turns values of 32 and 36 into bars of 2 and 6, so a 12.5% rise looks like a tripling. The labels can be true while the visual comparison is false.
  **Correction:** Start a bar chart at zero. If a truncated axis is genuinely needed for detail, label it clearly, say that it is truncated, and expect the reader to compare the printed values rather than the bar lengths. For a line graph, check the vertical range before describing the size of a change.

### Assessment and mark scheme

#### a1 · constructed-response

A survey of 200 learners records how they travel: bus 80, walk 50, car 40, cycle 30. Calculate the pie angles, explain what the pie does not show, and explain why a bar chart of the same data should start at zero.

**Model answer:** The total is 200. The angles are 80/200 × 360° = 144°, 50/200 × 360° = 90°, 40/200 × 360° = 72° and 30/200 × 360° = 54°, which sum to 360°. The pie shows each share of the 200, but it does not show the total or the counts: the largest sector means the largest share, not automatically the largest number unless the total is known and printed. A bar chart of the counts must start at zero because the bar's length encodes the count; starting at 60 would draw the 80 and 50 bars as 20 and -10, which is impossible, and would make the differences look far larger than they are. The zero baseline keeps the bars proportional to the counts, so the comparison the chart invites is the comparison the data support.

**Marking criteria:**

1. Uses the total 200 and obtains the four angles 144°, 90°, 72° and 54°.
2. Checks that the angles sum to 360°.
3. Explains that the pie shows shares, not counts, and that the total is not visible in the circle.
4. Explains that bar length encodes the count, so a non-zero baseline distorts the comparison, and gives the concrete effect of starting at 60.

#### a2 · short-answer

A pie chart shows that 25% of learners walk. The total is 80 learners. How many walk?

**Model answer:** 20

**Marking criteria:**

1. Converts the share to a fraction or decimal: 25% = 0.25.
2. Multiplies by the total: 0.25 × 80 = 20 learners.

#### a3 · short-answer

A bar chart's vertical axis starts at 100. Two bars are labelled 108 and 116. What is the true difference between the values?

**Model answer:** 8

**Marking criteria:**

1. Subtracts the two printed values rather than the drawn heights: 116 - 108.
2. Obtains 8, noting that the drawn heights are 16 and 8 if the baseline is 100.

#### a4 · structured

A survey of 40 learners records how they travel: 10 walk, 20 take the bus and 10 come by car. Complete the table of pie-chart angles.

**Model answer:** Each count is divided by the total 40 before multiplying by 360°: walking 10/40 × 360° = 90°, bus 20/40 × 360° = 180° and car 10/40 × 360° = 90°. The three angles add to 360°, which checks that the whole circle has been used.

**Marking criteria:**

1. Walking: 10/40 × 360° = 90°.
2. Bus: 20/40 × 360° = 180°.
3. Car: 10/40 × 360° = 90°.
4. States that the three angles must total 360°, the whole circle.

**Structured parts:**

- (a) Complete the angle for each category. Each cell is worth one mark. — 3 marks · response: table
- (b) Add your three angles. What must the total be? — 1 mark

### Transfer

**Two schools report their cycling. School A has 400 pupils and 40 cycle; School B has 250 pupils and 30 cycle. A headline says 'More cyclists at School B'. A second says 'Cycling is more common at School B'. Calculate the counts and the shares, and say which chart would support each headline. Then explain which claim is true, and what would reverse the second claim.**  
Why it transfers: The lesson reads one chart and computes one set of pie angles. Here the learner must compare two data sets with different totals, keep the count and the share apart, decide which representation supports which sentence, and then work out what would change the answer. The arithmetic is familiar; the separation of count from share, and the fact that a total can reverse a claim, are new.  
Answer: School A: 40 out of 400 is 40/400 = 10%. School B: 30 out of 250 is 30/250 = 12%. School B has the higher share, but School A has more cyclists in total (40 against 30). A bar chart of the counts supports 'more cyclists at A', because 40 is taller than 30; the pie charts, or a bar of percentages, support 'cycling is more common at B', because 12% is a larger share than 10%. So the first headline is false and the second is true, but they are answers to different questions: one is about counts, the other about rates. A pie alone cannot show the 40 against 30 without the totals printed. If School B had 500 pupils, 30 cyclists would be 6%, below School A's 10%, so the second headline would reverse while the counts stayed the same — the total is doing the work.

- **screen-reader:** Every chart is also given as a table or a list of values in the text, and each angle is written as a share and a number of degrees, so no answer depends on seeing the drawing.
- **low-vision:** Values and angles are set in the reading text at the learner's chosen size, and the lesson never relies on a small label inside a chart; the same numbers are in the paragraph beside it.
- **dyslexia:** The reading routine is three short questions — total, scale, missing — and each calculation is written as one operation per line so a reader can follow it without holding a whole table in mind.

## physics: Motion (`motion-graphs`)

**Why this lesson:** A graph, a plotted series and a follow-through read-off, with the gradient/area distinction.

**Curriculum:** GCSE Physics, forces and motion: distance–time and velocity–time graphs, gradient and area · Key Stage 3 to GCSE

**Prerequisites:** forces-basics, straight-lines

**Version:** 1.2.0 · **Declared status:** draft · **Reviews:** 0 · **Learner testing:** 0

### Objectives

- **Read the gradient of a distance–time graph as speed, and distinguish instantaneous speed from average speed over a whole journey.**  
  Evidence: For a journey that covers 8 m in the first 4 s and then rests for 3 s, the learner reads 2 m/s from the first gradient and 0 m/s from the flat section, then calculates the average speed over the whole journey from total distance divided by total time.
- **Read the gradient of a velocity–time graph as acceleration and the area under it as displacement, including negative values.**  
  Evidence: For a cyclist who reaches 8 m/s from rest in 4 s, the learner calculates an acceleration of 2 m/s² and a distance of 16 m from the triangular area, with the units stated.
- **Explain why a high flat line on a distance–time graph is not a fast object, and why a flat velocity–time line is not an object at rest.**  
  Evidence: The learner says the height of a distance–time graph is how far away the object is, so a high flat line means it is far away and stationary; a flat velocity–time line means the velocity is constant, so the object is still moving and its displacement continues to grow.

### Explanation

#### The axes are the first sentence of the graph

Before reading any number, name the quantity on each axis and its unit. A distance–time graph plots position against time, so its vertical axis is in metres and its horizontal axis is in seconds. A velocity–time graph plots velocity against time, so its vertical axis is in metres per second. The same rising line therefore means different things on the two graphs: on the first it is a fast object, and on the second it is an object whose velocity is increasing.
The shape of the line is read between two points, not at a single point. A straight line means the gradient is constant, so the quantity represented by that gradient stays the same. A curved line means the gradient changes, so speed or acceleration is changing. A flat line means the vertical quantity is not changing, which is not the same as the object being absent or the graph being empty.

#### Distance–time: the gradient is speed

On a distance–time graph, gradient is the change in distance divided by the change in time. Those are the units of speed, metres per second, so the gradient is the speed. A steep line means a large distance is covered in a short time, so the object is fast. A shallow line means the object is slow, and a flat line means its distance is not changing at all, so it is stationary.
For a journey with several stages, calculate the gradient of each straight section separately rather than joining the ends. A graph that rises, flattens and rises again has three different speeds, and the gradient of the whole line from start to finish is only the average velocity, not what happened at any particular moment. The average speed is a separate calculation: total distance travelled divided by total time taken. A return journey is the case that makes the difference unavoidable, because the distance travelled keeps increasing while the displacement can return to zero.

#### Velocity–time: the gradient is acceleration, the area is displacement

On a velocity–time graph, gradient is the change in velocity divided by the change in time, which is acceleration. A positive gradient means velocity is increasing in the positive direction; a negative gradient means it is decreasing, or becoming more negative. A flat line means the velocity is constant, so the acceleration is zero and the object is not at rest unless the flat line sits on the horizontal axis.
The area between the line and the time axis gives the displacement. Split the area into rectangles and triangles rather than looking for a single formula. A rectangle of height 8 m/s and width 6 s contributes 48 m; a triangle of height 8 m/s and base 4 s contributes 16 m. If the line crosses below the time axis, that area is negative displacement, so the object is moving back towards its starting point and the signed areas must be added rather than all counted as distance.

#### Signs, averages and the assumptions a graph makes

Speed is a scalar: it has size but no direction, so it is never negative. Velocity is a vector: its sign says which way the object moves. That is why a graph can show a negative velocity, and why average speed and average velocity can disagree. A runner who goes 100 m out and 100 m back in 50 s has an average speed of 4 m/s but an average velocity of zero, because the displacement is zero even though the distance travelled is 200 m.
The straight-line model assumes constant acceleration between the marked points, and it treats the motion as one-dimensional. Those assumptions are idealisations: a real car changes acceleration continuously, and a real road bends. The graph is still useful because it captures the part of the motion we care about, but an answer should say when the model is being used outside the range where it is a good approximation.

### Representations

**physics.equation** — The gradient of a straight line is the change in the vertical quantity divided by the change in the horizontal quantity. On a motion graph, the axes decide which physical quantity that gradient represents.

```text
gradient = change in vertical value / change in horizontal value
```

Caption: On a distance–time graph the gradient is speed in metres per second. On a velocity–time graph the gradient is acceleration in metres per second squared. The units are the clue: dividing metres by seconds gives metres per second.

**physics.equation** — The area under a velocity–time graph is the displacement. A rectangle contributes height times width, and a triangle contributes one half times base times height.

```text
displacement = area under velocity–time graph
```

Caption: A velocity of 8 m/s held for 6 s gives an area of 48 m. A velocity rising steadily from zero to 8 m/s over 4 s gives a triangle of area 16 m. The area's unit is metres because metres per second multiplied by seconds is metres.

**physics.units** — Every reading from a motion graph carries a unit, and the unit is how you check that you have read the right feature. A bare number cannot tell you whether it is a distance, a speed or an acceleration.

```text
distance: m · time: s · speed: m/s · velocity: m/s · acceleration: m/s²
```

Caption: Metres divided by seconds gives metres per second. A change in metres per second divided by seconds gives metres per second squared. If the unit does not match the question, the gradient or area has been read from the wrong graph.

**physics.model** — The two graphs answer different questions. A distance–time graph says where the object is; a velocity–time graph says how its velocity is changing and how far it has travelled.

```text
distance–time: height = position, gradient = speed, flat = stationary
velocity–time: height = velocity, gradient = acceleration, flat = constant velocity, area = displacement
```

Caption: A flat distance–time line means the object has stopped. A flat velocity–time line means the object is moving at a steady velocity. The same shape has opposite meanings because the vertical axes are different quantities.

### Worked example

**A walker moves 8 m away from a sensor in the first 4 s, rests for 3 s, then walks 6 m back towards the sensor in the next 3 s. Calculate the speed during each stage, the total distance, the displacement and the average speed for the whole 10 s.**

1. **For the first stage, gradient = change in distance / change in time = 8 m / 4 s = 2 m/s.** — The distance–time gradient is speed, and the first section is a straight line, so the walker's speed is constant at 2 m/s away from the sensor.
2. **The middle stage is flat, so its gradient is 0 m / 3 s = 0 m/s, which means the walker is stationary.** — A flat distance–time line means the distance from the sensor is not changing, so the walker is at rest for those 3 s.
3. **The final stage has gradient = 6 m / 3 s = 2 m/s back towards the sensor, so the velocity is negative if away is positive.** — The distance is falling, so the change is negative; the speed is 2 m/s but the direction is opposite to the first stage.
4. **Total distance = 8 m + 6 m = 14 m; displacement = 8 m − 6 m = 2 m away; average speed = 14 m / 10 s = 1.4 m/s.** — Distance counts both directions, while displacement counts the straight-line change from start to finish. Average speed uses the total distance, not the displacement.

Answer: The speeds are 2 m/s, 0 m/s and 2 m/s; the total distance is 14 m, the displacement is 2 m away from the sensor, and the average speed is 1.4 m/s.

**A cyclist accelerates uniformly from rest to 8 m/s in 4 s, then travels at 8 m/s for 6 s. Calculate the acceleration during the first stage and the total displacement shown by the velocity–time graph.**

1. **For the first stage, acceleration = change in velocity / change in time = 8 m/s / 4 s = 2 m/s².** — The velocity–time gradient is acceleration. The velocity rises by 8 m/s in 4 s, so the acceleration is 2 metres per second squared.
2. **The first stage's area is a triangle: one half times base times height = one half times 4 s times 8 m/s = 16 m.** — The velocity rises steadily from zero, so the area under that section is a triangle rather than a rectangle.
3. **The second stage's area is a rectangle: 8 m/s times 6 s = 48 m. Total displacement = 16 m + 48 m = 64 m.** — A constant velocity for 6 s contributes height times width, and the two areas add because both stages move in the same direction.

Answer: The acceleration is 2 m/s² and the total displacement is 64 m.

### Misconceptions

- **Wrong belief:** The height of a distance–time graph tells you how fast the object is moving.
  **Why it fails:** Height is the object's distance from the starting point, not its speed. A high flat line means the object is far away and stationary, while a line starting at zero and rising steeply means the object is close but moving quickly. The speed is the gradient, which is how steeply the line rises, not how high it has reached.
  **Correction:** Read speed from the gradient of the distance–time line. Read position from its height. A flat line at any height means zero speed.
- **Wrong belief:** A flat velocity–time graph means the object has stopped.
  **Why it fails:** A flat velocity–time line means the velocity is not changing, so the object is moving at a constant velocity. It has stopped only if the line lies on the horizontal axis at zero. A flat line above the axis shows a steady speed, and the area beneath it continues to add displacement every second.
  **Correction:** A flat velocity–time line means zero acceleration, not zero velocity. Check the height of the line to see whether the constant velocity is zero or non-zero.
- **Wrong belief:** Negative acceleration always means the object is slowing down.
  **Why it fails:** Acceleration is a vector. A negative acceleration means the acceleration points in the negative direction. An object already moving in the negative direction speeds up when its acceleration is negative, while an object moving in the positive direction slows down. The sign alone does not tell you whether the speed is rising or falling; you need the direction of motion as well.
  **Correction:** Compare the signs of velocity and acceleration. If they point the same way, the object speeds up; if they point in opposite directions, it slows down. Negative acceleration is not a synonym for deceleration.

## chemistry: Acids, bases & salts (`acids-bases`)

**Why this lesson:** A practical-adjacent topic whose assessment asks for explanation and ion reasoning.

**Curriculum:** GCSE Chemistry, acids, bases and salts: proton transfer, pH, neutralisation, strong and weak acids · Key Stage 3 to GCSE

**Prerequisites:** compounds-mixtures, ionic-bonding

**Version:** 1.0.0 · **Declared status:** draft · **Reviews:** 0 · **Learner testing:** 0

### Objectives

- **Classify an acid as a proton donor and a base as a proton acceptor, and distinguish an alkali from a base.**  
  Evidence: Given ammonia, sodium hydroxide and calcium carbonate, the learner places each on the acid–base axis and explains the classification by what accepts H⁺, not by whether the formula contains OH.
- **Interpret pH as a logarithmic measure of H⁺ concentration and calculate the effect of a tenfold dilution.**  
  Evidence: The learner states that pH 3 has one hundred times the H⁺ concentration of pH 5, and that diluting a strong acid tenfold raises its pH by one unit, not by a factor of ten.
- **Predict the salt from an acid–base reaction, write a balanced equation, and separate strong from concentrated using ionisation and conductivity evidence.**  
  Evidence: The learner writes a balanced equation for sulfuric acid and potassium hydroxide, identifies the spectator ions, and explains why 0.1 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid is a weaker conductor than 0.1 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid.

### Explanation

#### An acid donates a proton; a base accepts one

In aqueous solution, an acid releases hydrogen ions, H⁺. A hydrogen ion is a proton, so the modern definition is broader than 'contains H': an acid is a proton donor. Hydrogen chloride ionises completely in water, HCl → H⁺ + Cl⁻, and nitric acid behaves in the same way. A base is a proton acceptor. Hydroxide ions accept H⁺ to form water, OH⁻ + H⁺ → H₂O, which is why soluble hydroxides such as sodium hydroxide are alkaline. Ammonia is also a base: NH₃ + H⁺ → NH₄⁺, even though its formula contains no OH.
An alkali is a base that dissolves in water. All alkalis are bases, but not all bases are alkalis: copper(II) oxide and calcium carbonate are bases that do not dissolve to give an alkaline solution. Bases do not have to contain hydroxide either. Metal oxides, hydroxides and carbonates all accept protons, and the reaction with an acid produces a salt. Keeping the definition at the level of proton transfer stops the formula from being treated as the definition.

#### pH is logarithmic, so one unit is a factor of ten

At 25 °C, a neutral aqueous solution has pH 7. Values below 7 are acidic and values above 7 are alkaline. The number is not a linear amount of acid: pH is defined as −log₁₀[H⁺], where [H⁺] is the concentration of hydrogen ions in mol dm⁻³. A solution of pH 3 therefore has 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ of H⁺, and a solution of pH 5 has 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. pH 3 is one hundred times more concentrated in H⁺ than pH 5, because the difference of two pH units corresponds to two factors of ten.
This is why pH 0 does not mean 'no acid' and pH 14 does not mean 'the most dangerous substance'. It also explains why a dilute strong acid can have a higher pH than a concentrated weak acid: pH measures the H⁺ that is actually free in solution, not the amount of acid put into the bottle. Universal indicator gives an approximate colour, while a calibrated pH meter gives a number. At temperatures other than 25 °C the neutral pH is not exactly 7, so 'neutral' is defined by equal H⁺ and OH⁻ concentrations, not by the number alone.

#### Neutralisation transfers a proton and leaves a salt

An acid reacting with a hydroxide base produces a salt and water: acid + base → salt + water. The cation of the salt comes from the base and the anion comes from the acid. Hydrochloric acid gives chloride salts, sulfuric acid gives sulfates, nitric acid gives nitrates, and ethanoic acid gives ethanoates. The balanced equation must respect the charges as well as the atoms: sulfuric acid has two protons to donate, so it needs two hydroxide ions, as in H₂SO₄ + 2NaOH → Na₂SO₄ + 2H₂O.
The essential change is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O. Sodium and sulfate ions are spectators: they are present before and after and do not change. Metal oxides and carbonates are also bases, but they do not fit the simple 'salt + water' pattern: a carbonate also releases carbon dioxide, as in 2HCl + Na₂CO₃ → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂. A neutralisation reaction is not the same as a neutral solution. The mixture is at pH 7 only when the acid and base have reacted in exactly the right proportions and both are strong; a weak acid with a strong base can leave an alkaline solution at the equivalence point.

#### Strong and concentrated are independent axes

Strong describes how completely an acid ionises in water. Hydrochloric and nitric acids are strong: essentially every molecule donates its proton. Ethanoic acid is weak: only a small fraction of its molecules ionise, so the solution contains mostly intact molecules and relatively few H⁺ ions. Concentrated describes how much acid is dissolved per unit volume. It says nothing about the fraction that ionises. A solution can be strong and dilute, strong and concentrated, weak and dilute, or weak and concentrated.
The evidence separates the two ideas. At the same concentration, the strong acid has more mobile ions, so its solution conducts electricity better and usually has a lower pH. A weak acid can be made more concentrated and become more acidic, but it is still weak: dilution does not turn a strong acid weak, and concentration does not turn a weak acid strong. When a label says 'strong', ask what is fully ionised; when it says 'concentrated', ask how much is present per litre. A hazard assessment needs both, along with the actual pH.

### Representations

**chemistry.equation** — Hydrochloric acid neutralises sodium hydroxide. The sodium and chloride ions are spectators; the proton and hydroxide ion combine to form water.

```text
HCl + NaOH → NaCl + H₂O   ·   H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O
```

Caption: The full equation accounts for every ion. The ionic equation shows only the species that change. Neither equation says the final solution is neutral: that depends on the amounts mixed.

**chemistry.particle-model** — Two solutions of the same concentration. Hydrochloric acid is fully ionised, so it supplies many H⁺ ions and conducts well. Ethanoic acid is only partly ionised, so most of its molecules remain intact, fewer H⁺ ions are free, and the solution conducts less.

```text
0.1 mol dm⁻³ HCl: fully ionised, pH ≈ 1   ·   0.1 mol dm⁻³ CH₃COOH: partly ionised, pH ≈ 3
```

Caption: The difference is the fraction ionised, not the amount dissolved. Both solutions are 0.1 mol dm⁻³, so concentration is held constant while strength changes.

**chemistry.calculation** — Diluting a strong acid by a known factor lowers the H⁺ concentration by that factor. Because pH is logarithmic, a tenfold dilution raises the pH by one unit; a hundredfold dilution raises it by two.

```text
pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]   ·   10 cm³ of pH 2 diluted to 1000 cm³ is a 100-fold dilution, so pH 2 → pH 4
```

Caption: This calculation assumes the acid is strong and the dilution is well above the point where water's own ionisation matters. Near pH 7, adding water cannot simply be treated as another factor of ten.

**chemistry.formula** — The acid's anion names the salt family. The base's cation names the first part of the salt. Charges decide how many of each ion are needed, so the formula is built from the ions rather than copied from the acid.

```text
HCl → chloride   ·   H₂SO₄ → sulfate   ·   HNO₃ → nitrate   ·   CH₃COOH → ethanoate
```

Caption: Sulfuric acid can donate two protons, so a sulfate salt needs two singly charged cations: KOH gives K₂SO₄, while NaOH gives Na₂SO₄.

### Worked example

**Write the balanced equation and the ionic equation for the reaction between sulfuric acid and potassium hydroxide.**

1. **Identify the ions: H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ from the acid; K⁺ and OH⁻ from the base.** — Sulfuric acid can donate two protons, so its anion carries a 2− charge. Potassium hydroxide supplies one K⁺ for every OH⁻. Writing the ions first prevents the formula being assembled by guesswork.
2. **Combine one sulfate ion with two potassium ions, and balance the proton transfer with two hydroxide ions: H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O.** — The two negative charges on sulfate need two positive potassium ions. The two H⁺ from sulfuric acid need two OH⁻ to become two water molecules. Every atom and every charge now balances.
3. **Remove the spectator ions to obtain H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O.** — Potassium and sulfate ions appear unchanged on both sides. The ionic equation keeps the reaction that actually happens and shows why every acid–hydroxide neutralisation has the same core.

Answer: H₂SO₄ + 2KOH → K₂SO₄ + 2H₂O; the ionic equation is H⁺ + OH⁻ → H₂O, with K⁺ and SO₄²⁻ as spectators.

**10 cm³ of a strong acid solution of pH 2 is diluted with pure water to a final volume of 1000 cm³. Calculate the new pH.**

1. **Find the dilution factor: 1000 ÷ 10 = 100.** — The same amount of acid is spread through a hundred times the volume, so its concentration falls by a factor of one hundred. The dilution factor is a ratio of volumes, not a subtraction.
2. **Reduce the H⁺ concentration by that factor: from 10⁻² to 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³.** — pH 2 means [H⁺] = 10⁻². Dividing by 100 subtracts two from the exponent, giving 10⁻⁴. The acid is strong, so the H⁺ concentration is taken as the acid's concentration for this school-level calculation.
3. **Convert back to pH: −log₁₀(10⁻⁴) = 4.** — Each factor of ten changes pH by one unit, and there were two factors of ten, so the pH rises by two. The answer is pH 4, not pH 200 or pH 7: dilution lowers concentration but does not remove every acid particle.

Answer: The new pH is 4.

### Misconceptions

- **Wrong belief:** A strong acid is the same thing as a concentrated acid.
  **Why it fails:** Strength is the fraction of acid molecules that ionise; concentration is how many moles are dissolved per litre. A 0.001 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid solution is strong because it is fully ionised, but it is very dilute and has pH 3. A concentrated ethanoic acid solution can have a lower pH than that while remaining weak. The two words answer different questions.
  **Correction:** Describe both axes separately: 'strong' means fully ionised, 'concentrated' means a large amount per litre. Then quote the pH or the concentration as evidence rather than using either word as a substitute for the other.
- **Wrong belief:** pH is a linear amount, so pH 4 is twice as acidic as pH 2.
  **Why it fails:** The pH scale is logarithmic. pH 2 means [H⁺] = 10⁻² mol dm⁻³ and pH 4 means 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³, so pH 2 has one hundred times the H⁺ concentration of pH 4, not twice. Reversing the comparison is just as wrong: the lower pH is the more acidic solution.
  **Correction:** Turn each pH into a power of ten before comparing. A difference of one pH unit is a factor of ten in H⁺ concentration; a difference of two units is a factor of one hundred.
- **Wrong belief:** Every base contains OH⁻, and every neutralisation leaves a neutral solution.
  **Why it fails:** Ammonia is a base because it accepts H⁺ to form NH₄⁺, even though it contains no hydroxide. A base is defined by proton acceptance, and an alkali is simply a base that dissolves. Separately, a neutralisation reaction can leave an acidic or alkaline solution if the amounts are not at the equivalence point, or if a weak acid is mixed with a strong base.
  **Correction:** Classify bases by what they do to H⁺, not by whether OH appears in the formula. Treat 'neutralisation' as the reaction and 'neutral' as a statement about the final pH; test the result rather than assuming it.

## biology: Photosynthesis (`photosynthesis`)

**Why this lesson:** A gap-fill and matching assessment alongside a constructed explanation of limiting factors.

**Curriculum:** GCSE Biology, bioenergetics: photosynthesis and limiting factors · Key Stage 3 to GCSE

**Prerequisites:** enzymes

**Version:** 1.0.0 · **Declared status:** draft · **Reviews:** 0 · **Learner testing:** 0

### Objectives

- **State the reactants, products and location of photosynthesis, and explain what the plant does with each product.**  
  Evidence: The learner names carbon dioxide and water as reactants, glucose and oxygen as products, places the process in the chloroplast, and says glucose is used for growth, storage as starch or respiration, while oxygen leaves the leaf.
- **Read a rate-versus-light-intensity graph and identify which factor is limiting at a given point.**  
  Evidence: At the steep part of the curve the learner names light intensity as limiting; at the plateau the learner says another factor, such as carbon dioxide concentration or temperature, has taken over and proposes how to test that.
- **Explain why a plant both photosynthesises and respires, and why the net gas exchange of a leaf depends on which rate is greater.**  
  Evidence: The learner states that respiration runs all the time in every living cell, while photosynthesis runs only in the light, so in darkness a leaf takes in oxygen and releases carbon dioxide and in bright light the reverse is net.

### Explanation

#### The reaction, and where it happens

Photosynthesis takes carbon dioxide and water and uses light energy to build glucose, releasing oxygen. In symbols, six carbon dioxide plus six water give one glucose plus six oxygen. The reaction happens in chloroplasts, which contain the green pigment chlorophyll that absorbs light. A plant without chloroplasts — a root cell, for example — cannot photosynthesise, which is why roots depend on the leaves for sugar.
The glucose is not simply fuel. Some is used in respiration, some is converted to starch for storage, some becomes cellulose for cell walls, and some is combined with nitrogen to make amino acids. The oxygen is a by-product of splitting water and leaves the leaf by diffusion.

#### Rate is set by whichever factor is in shortest supply

Increasing light intensity increases the rate, but only until something else runs out. On a graph of rate against light, the rising section means light is limiting: more light gives more photosynthesis. The flat section means light is no longer limiting; carbon dioxide concentration or temperature has become the bottleneck, and adding more light changes nothing.
This is the same saturation idea that governs enzymes, and it is the reason a greenhouse raises carbon dioxide as well as letting light in. Temperature is a limiting factor in a different way: it speeds up the enzyme-controlled reactions, but beyond an optimum the enzymes denature and the rate falls sharply.

#### Plants respire too, all the time

Respiration releases energy from glucose and happens in every living cell, in darkness as well as light. Photosynthesis only happens where there is chlorophyll and light. So a leaf is running both processes at once during the day, and only respiration at night.
That is why gas exchange changes direction. In darkness, oxygen diffuses in and carbon dioxide diffuses out. In bright light, photosynthesis usually exceeds respiration, so carbon dioxide diffuses in and oxygen diffuses out. At the compensation point the two rates are equal and there is no net exchange, which is a fact about a ratio rather than about the plant switching processes on and off.

#### Testing the claim, not just stating it

A pondweed in water releases oxygen bubbles. Moving the lamp further away lowers the bubble count, and plotting rate against distance shows the falling curve. But bubbles are a proxy: they count a product of photosynthesis, and the count is only meaningful if everything else — temperature, carbon dioxide concentration, the plant's health — is held constant.
A claim about a limiting factor is a claim about a controlled comparison. Vary one factor, hold the rest, and look for the point where the rate stops responding. Without the control, a change in rate could be attributed to the wrong cause, which is the single most common weakness in answers to this kind of question.

### Representations

**biology.process** — Light is absorbed by chlorophyll in the chloroplast, which drives the splitting of water and the fixing of carbon dioxide into glucose; oxygen leaves the leaf by diffusion because its concentration inside the leaf is higher than outside.

```text
6CO₂ + 6H₂O → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
```

Caption: The arrow is driven by light energy absorbed by chlorophyll. Remove the light and the process stops, even though every reactant is still present.

**biology.structure-function** — A palisade cell is packed with chloroplasts near the top of the leaf, because that position and density let it absorb the most light per unit area before the light is intercepted by cells below.

```text
palisade mesophyll: chloroplast-dense, upper layer
```

Caption: The structure exists for the function. A leaf that put its chloroplasts at the bottom would absorb far less light for the same cost of building the leaf.

**biology.structure-function** — A leaf has stomata on its underside and air spaces between spongy mesophyll cells, so that carbon dioxide can diffuse in and oxygen out along a short, moist path.

```text
stoma + air space: short diffusion path, large surface area
```

Caption: Diffusion needs a gradient and a short path. The leaf's internal architecture supplies both, which is why a thick, dry leaf would photosynthesise poorly even in full sun.

**biology.experiment** — Pondweed is placed at increasing distances from a lamp and the oxygen bubbles are counted for one minute at each distance. Temperature and carbon dioxide concentration are kept constant, and the plant is left to settle before each count.

```text
distance 10, 20, 30, 40 cm · bubbles per minute recorded · other factors controlled
```

Caption: Bubble count is a proxy for rate. The controlled variables are what make a fall in the count attributable to distance rather than to the plant warming up or running out of carbon dioxide.

### Worked example

**A student measures the rate of photosynthesis of pondweed at increasing light intensities. The rate rises and then levels off. Explain what is happening at each part of the graph and how the student could test the explanation.**

1. **On the rising section, identify light intensity as the limiting factor, because increasing it increases the rate.** — A factor is limiting when changing it changes the rate. The rise is direct evidence that light is the factor in shortest supply at those points.
2. **At the plateau, conclude that light is no longer limiting and that another factor has taken over — most likely carbon dioxide concentration or temperature.** — The rate has stopped responding to the variable being changed. That only happens when a different factor has become the bottleneck, so the plateau is evidence about a second factor rather than about light.
3. **Propose a controlled test: repeat the experiment with a higher carbon dioxide concentration, keeping light intensity, temperature and the plant the same, and see whether the plateau is higher.** — The claim is that carbon dioxide is now limiting. Testing it means changing only that factor; if the plateau rises, the claim is supported, and if it does not, temperature is the better candidate.

Answer: Light is limiting on the rising section and carbon dioxide (or temperature) is limiting at the plateau; raising carbon dioxide while holding everything else constant should raise the plateau if that is the limiting factor.

**A leaf is sealed in a transparent chamber. In bright light the carbon dioxide concentration in the chamber falls; in darkness it rises. Explain both observations using the two processes happening in the leaf.**

1. **Identify that photosynthesis takes in carbon dioxide and respiration releases it, and that both are happening in the leaf.** — The observation cannot be explained by one process alone. Both are always available; what differs between light and dark is the rate of photosynthesis.
2. **In bright light, conclude that the rate of photosynthesis exceeds the rate of respiration, so the net effect is uptake of carbon dioxide.** — Net exchange is a difference between two rates, not a switch. Photosynthesis is using carbon dioxide faster than respiration is producing it.
3. **In darkness, conclude that photosynthesis has stopped while respiration continues, so the net effect is release of carbon dioxide.** — Respiration does not depend on light. With photosynthesis at zero, the only process moving carbon dioxide is respiration, which releases it.

Answer: In light, photosynthesis outpaces respiration and the leaf takes up carbon dioxide; in darkness photosynthesis stops while respiration continues, so the leaf releases carbon dioxide.

### Misconceptions

- **Wrong belief:** Plants photosynthesise instead of respiring, so they only take in carbon dioxide.
  **Why it fails:** Respiration happens in every living cell, continuously, including in plants and including in the light. Photosynthesis is an additional process, not a replacement. If a plant did not respire, it could not release the energy stored in the glucose it has just made.
  **Correction:** Both processes run during the day; only respiration runs at night. What you observe as gas exchange is the NET of the two rates, which is why the direction can reverse.
- **Wrong belief:** The flat part of a light-intensity graph means photosynthesis has stopped.
  **Why it fails:** A plateau is a rate that has stopped rising, not a rate that has fallen to zero. Photosynthesis is still happening at the plateau — often faster than at any earlier point on the graph. What has stopped is the response to light.
  **Correction:** Read the plateau as a change of limiting factor. The rate is set by something else now, and the graph tells you that increasing light further will not help.
- **Wrong belief:** A plant gets its food from the soil.
  **Why it fails:** Soil supplies water and mineral ions, not energy. The mass of a tree comes overwhelmingly from carbon dioxide in the air, fixed into glucose by photosynthesis. A plant in distilled water with light and air can still grow; a plant with soil and no light cannot.
  **Correction:** Separate raw materials from energy. Water and minerals are raw materials absorbed by roots; the energy and the carbon come from light and air. Glucose is the plant's food, and the plant makes it.

## computing: What is code? (`what-is-code`)

**Why this lesson:** Code tracing and debugging, with an explanation of what a trace proves.

**Curriculum:** GCSE Computer Science, programming: sequence, selection and iteration · Key Stage 3 to GCSE

**Prerequisites:** none declared

**Version:** 1.0.0 · **Declared status:** draft · **Reviews:** 0 · **Learner testing:** 0

### Objectives

- **Read a short program and predict its output without running it.**  
  Evidence: Given a loop that adds the numbers one to five, the learner states that the output is fifteen and can point to the line where the running total changes.
- **Trace a program's variables one line at a time and record their values.**  
  Evidence: The learner produces a table whose rows are the loop iterations and whose columns are the loop variable and the running total, and the final row matches the printed output.
- **Find a fault in a program by comparing the trace with the intended behaviour, and repair it.**  
  Evidence: Given a loop that starts at zero or stops one iteration early, the learner names the line, states what it should be, and explains why the change fixes the output.

### Explanation

#### A program is a sequence of instructions

Code is written as a list of statements, and the computer executes them one after another, top to bottom, unless a statement changes the order. Each statement does one small thing: stores a value, adds two numbers, compares two values, prints something. Nothing happens by implication — if the program does not say it, it does not happen.
That is why the same instructions in a different order produce a different result. It is also why a program that runs is not necessarily correct: the computer will faithfully execute a wrong instruction.

#### Variables are named boxes

A variable is a name for a stored value. Writing total = 0 puts zero in a box labelled total. Later, total = total + 5 reads the value in the box, adds five, and puts the new value back in the same box. The equals sign here is not a mathematical claim that both sides are equal; it is an instruction to store.
Understanding that distinction is what makes a trace possible. After total = total + 5 you can say exactly what is in the box, and that is the state of the program at that moment.

#### Selection and iteration change which lines run

An if statement runs a block only when a condition is true. A for loop runs a block once for each value in a sequence. Between them they are why a program can be short and still do a great deal of work.
A for loop is not a vague repetition. It names a variable, gives it each value in turn, and runs the body once per value. Knowing how many times the body runs is the difference between predicting the output and guessing it.

#### A trace is how you find out what a program actually did

A trace is a table with one row per iteration and one column per variable that changes. Fill it in by hand, following the program line by line. At the end, the last row tells you the values the program finished with, which is what the output must be.
Tracing is also the debugging method. If the intended answer is fifteen and the trace ends at ten, the difference is not a mystery: the last iteration did not run, or the running total started in the wrong place. The trace localises the fault to a line, which a guess cannot do.

### Representations

**computing.code** — A program that adds the numbers from one to five. Read it as five instructions plus a loop, not as a sentence.

```text
total = 0
for n in range(1, 6):
    total = total + n
print(total)
```

Caption: The loop variable n takes the values one, two, three, four and five. The running total is updated once per value, and the print happens after the loop.

**computing.trace** — The trace of the program above. Each row is one pass through the loop; the last row is the state when the loop ends.

```text
n = 1  total = 1
n = 2  total = 3
n = 3  total = 6
n = 4  total = 10
n = 5  total = 15
```

Caption: The total after the final iteration is fifteen, so that is what the program prints. If your prediction is different, the disagreement is in one of these five rows.

**computing.test** — Three tests for a program that is meant to add the numbers from one to n. One normal case, one boundary case and one invalid case, because a program that works for five may fail at zero.

```text
n = 5 → 15
n = 1 → 1
n = 0 → 0
```

Caption: The boundary case is the one that catches a loop written as range(1, n) instead of range(1, n + 1): it returns zero for n equal to one.

**computing.debug** — A faulty version of the same program. The loop stops one value early, so the last addition never happens.

```text
total = 0
for n in range(1, 5):
    total = total + n
print(total)
```

Caption: The output is ten rather than fifteen. The fault is the upper bound of the range, not the addition and not the print.

### Worked example

**Predict the output of this program without running it:

total = 0
for n in range(1, 6):
    total = total + n
print(total)**

1. **Work out how many times the loop body runs: range(1, 6) gives one, two, three, four and five, so five times.** — The upper bound in a range is exclusive in Python. Reading it as inclusive gives four iterations and a wrong total, which is the most common prediction error in this shape.
2. **Trace the running total: after one it is one, after two it is three, after three it is six, after four it is ten, after five it is fifteen.** — Each iteration adds the current loop value to what is already in the box. Writing the intermediate values down is what makes the final answer checkable rather than remembered.
3. **Read the print statement: it is outside the loop, so it prints once, after the last iteration, giving fifteen.** — Indentation decides which lines are inside the loop. A print inside the loop would print five times, once per iteration.

Answer: The program prints 15.

**This program is meant to add the numbers from one to five but prints ten. Find the fault and repair it:

total = 0
for n in range(1, 5):
    total = total + n
print(total)**

1. **Trace the program as written: n takes one to four, giving totals one, three, six and ten.** — A trace turns 'it is wrong' into 'it stops after four'. Debugging starts from what the program did, not from what it was meant to do.
2. **Compare the trace with the intended behaviour: the addition is correct and the print is correct, but the last iteration is missing.** — Two of the three parts of the program behave correctly, which narrows the fault to the loop bound. Changing the addition would break a part that already works.
3. **Repair the range to range(1, 6), then re-trace: the final total is fifteen, which is the intended output.** — An exclusive upper bound must be one more than the last value you want. Re-tracing confirms the repair rather than assuming it.

Answer: Change range(1, 5) to range(1, 6). The output becomes 15.

### Misconceptions

- **Wrong belief:** The computer understands what you meant, so small mistakes in the code will be worked out.
  **Why it fails:** A computer executes exactly the instructions it is given. There is no step that checks intent. A loop that runs four times instead of five is not a rough approximation of the intended program; it is a different program that happens to look similar.
  **Correction:** Read the program as written, not as intended. If the code does not say it, it does not happen — which is what makes a trace more reliable than intuition.
- **Wrong belief:** A program that runs without an error message is correct.
  **Why it fails:** An error message only reports that the program could not run. A program that produces the wrong answer runs perfectly. Ten is a perfectly valid output of the faulty loop, and the computer has no way to know that fifteen was wanted.
  **Correction:** Correctness is tested against expected outputs, not against the absence of crashes. Test a normal case, a boundary case and an invalid case.
- **Wrong belief:** The equals sign in total = total + 5 is a mathematical equation.
  **Why it fails:** Read as an equation, total would have to equal itself plus five, which is impossible. The statement is not a claim about equality; it is an instruction to evaluate the right-hand side and store the result in the named box.
  **Correction:** Read assignment as 'becomes'. The right-hand side is calculated first using the current value, and the result replaces what was in the variable.
