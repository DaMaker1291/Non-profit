# Chemistry modules compared — energetics, mole calculations and syllabus reach

**Reviewed:** 7 November 2026

**Scope:** public product pages and openly available course pages, plus the published
syllabus documents linked below. No paid account was created, so “what a learner sees
inside the product” is limited to what the vendor publishes publicly. Nothing here is a
claim about learning efficacy; it is an audit of teaching shape and coverage.

The question was threefold:

1. how other learning products teach **energetics** and **mole/calculation** chemistry;
2. whether OpenMind can teach a learner from **any country, against their own syllabus**;
3. why OpenMind’s lessons read as **one block of generated text**, and what changed.

## 1. What the comparison found

The strongest products do not teach a calculation as a paragraph of explanation. They
separate five jobs:

| Job | What good products do | What a wall of text fails to do |
|---|---|---|
| **Set up the idea** | a concrete situation before the formula | buries the reason for the formula |
| **Name the quantities** | a symbol table with units | leaves `n`, `m`, `Mᵣ`, `c` and `V` undefined |
| **Show the method** | numbered moves, one decision per move | makes the learner infer the order |
| **Work an example** | substitute real numbers, keep units visible | states a result without the working |
| **Name the trap** | “this is what a sign/unit slip looks like” | corrects nothing in particular |

That pattern is consistent across textbooks, exam-revision sites, virtual labs and
question banks. The differences are in **who supplies the sequence** and **how much
feedback is specific to the learner’s wrong move**.

**syllabus documents** (AQA, SACE, CISCE). A single category would have hidden the
different jobs a lesson has to do.

### What the best calculation teaching has in common

- **A named relationship before a number.** `n = m ÷ Mᵣ`, `c = n ÷ V` and
  `ΔH = Σ(broken) − Σ(formed)` are shown as relationships, then rearranged.
- **A unit check.** The strongest explanations make the unit part of the reasoning:
  `g ÷ g/mol = mol`; `cm³ ÷ 1000 = dm³`. This is where most learner errors are caught.
- **A ratio, not a mass ratio.** The coefficients in a balanced equation count particles
  or moles. Multiplying a mass by a coefficient is the characteristic wrong move.
- **A limiting-reagent decision.** The limiting reactant is the one whose amount divided
  by its coefficient is smallest, not simply the one with the smaller mass.
- **An energy ledger.** Bond breaking costs; bond making pays. The sign of ΔH belongs to
  the system, not to the thermometer.
- **A profile as a picture, not a second formula.** Eₐ is the barrier from reactants to
  the peak; ΔH is the difference between reactant and product levels. A catalyst lowers
  the first and leaves the second unchanged.

OpenMind already had the **questions** for these ideas. What it did not have for
`moles-calcs` and `energy-changes` was an authored lesson with that shape.

## 2. What changed in OpenMind

### A structured lesson, not a longer paragraph

`LessonPlan` now has an optional `sections` field (`lib/lesson-plans.ts`):

```ts
interface LessonSection {
  heading: string;
  body: readonly string[];
  formula?: string;
  steps?: readonly string[];
  table?: { columns; rows; caption? };
}
```

The two chemistry lessons use six sections each. The renderer
(`app/learn/[subject]/[concept]/page.tsx`) gives every section a number and a heading,
sets prose at a readable measure, and gives formulas, tables and methods their own
blocks. Plans without `sections` keep the old renderer, so this is an additive change,
not a rewrite of the other 133 concepts. The offline twin (`docs/app.js`, `docs/app.css`)
renders the same shape, because a formatting fix that exists only in the React app is
not a fix for the learner on the low-bandwidth build.

### `moles-calcs` — six sections

1. A mole is a count, not a mass — Avogadro’s constant, with a symbol/unit table.
2. Mass to moles — `n = m / Mᵣ`, the rearrangement, and a three-step unit check.
3. Concentration — `c = n / V`, the cm³-to-dm³ conversion, and a “given/use/result” table.
4. The balanced equation supplies the ratio — coefficients count particles, not grams.
5. Limiting reagent — the amount ÷ coefficient test, worked with 4 mol H₂ and 1 mol O₂.
6. The four-line method — balance, convert, ratio, convert back.

It has three worked examples (NaOH; a titration volume; CaCO₃ decomposition), three
traps with the reason each fails, five defined terms and a three-point recall check.

### `energy-changes` — six sections

1. Enthalpy is the energy ledger — `ΔH = H(products) − H(reactants)`, sign table.
2. Breaking costs, making pays — `ΔH = Σ(broken) − Σ(formed)`.
3. A bond-energy calculation — H₂ + Cl₂ → 2HCl, with a bond-energy table and the
   arithmetic laid out in two lines.
4. Reading a reaction profile — Eₐ versus ΔH; a catalyst lowers Eₐ only.
5. Calorimetry — `q = m c ΔT`, then `ΔH = −q / n`, with four numbered moves.
6. Signs and units — the three checks before writing an answer.

It has two worked examples (bond energies; calorimetry), three traps, five terms and a
three-point recap. Its figure is a **schematic** reaction profile: the caption says it is
not to scale, and the ΔH in the drawing is the value the lesson calculated (−185 kJ/mol),
while the activation height is illustrative. The figure geometry is in `lib/figures.ts`
and is asserted in `scripts/science-figure-check.mjs`; the new lesson-shape gate is
`scripts/chemistry-lesson-check.mjs`.

### What is deliberately not claimed

Both plans say `review: "unreviewed"` and `provenance: "authored"`. They are **not** in
the pilot sequence and are not presented as reviewed curriculum. The catalogue now
reports them separately from pilot plans. The page prints the review state beside
“used for”, so a learner is told what they are reading.

## 3. Country and syllabus coverage

### The structural answer

The curriculum registry (`lib/curricula/registry.ts`) records **which education systems a
country runs**, not a single “country → syllabus” shortcut. The current gate reports:

| Measure | Current value |
|---|---|
| Country choices offered | 71 |
| Countries with a national route | 70 |
| Explicitly unmapped choices | 1 (`Prefer not to say`) |
| National pathways | 71 (Cameroon has two systems) |
| Specifications (courses) | 91 |
| National / international / independent specifications | 88 / 2 / 1 |
| Specifications whose chemistry scope includes `moles-calcs` | 89 of 91 |
| Specifications whose chemistry scope includes `energy-changes` | 74 of 91 |
| Specifications whose chemistry scope includes `equations-stoich` | 89 of 91 |
| Course mappings with a published document on file | 2 |
| Those with a named subject reviewer | 0 |

The difference between 89 and 91 is not a failure: two courses do not carry that
chemistry scope. The difference between 74 and 89 is mostly lower-secondary courses that
stop before enthalpy. **A course’s scope is derived from its stage ranges and exclusions;
it is not the same claim as a specialist having read the syllabus.** That is the next
distinction, and it is the one the word “credible” depends on.

### The honest limitation

OpenMind can **offer a course for 70 countries and teach the common chemistry concepts**
in those courses. It cannot yet say “verified against every national syllabus”:

- only **two** specifications have a document-level mapping on file
  (`lib/curricula/mappings.ts`: UK GCSE and Indian CBSE);
- **zero** mappings have a named reviewer (`reviewer: null`), so every pathway stays at
  `mappingReview: "mapped"`, not `reviewed` or `verified`;
- 18 pathways have no structural source on file — 16 inherited national routes plus the
  international and independent routes — and the review ladder in
  `docs/CURRICULUM_REGISTRY.md` says so rather than upgrading the label;
- the same authored lesson is served to a Foundation learner and an IB Higher Level
  learner. Tier changes the **practice target**, not the lesson depth. That is a real
  limitation for a top-tier learner, and it is the next content job, not something the
  formatting change solved.

So the accurate statement is:

> **Any country:** structurally reachable for 70 countries, with international and
> independent routes for the rest. **Any syllabus:** not yet verified. Coverage is
> recorded, mapped at two documents, and unreviewed everywhere.

That is weaker than “teaches every syllabus” and stronger than “UK-only”. The repository’s
own rule is the right one: do not let a review label imply a review that did not happen.

### What would make the claim “verified for this syllabus” true

1. Read the current official specification, not a revision guide.
2. Record the verbatim heading for each concept, as `lib/curricula/mappings.ts` already
   requires.
3. Have a subject specialist sign `reviewer` and `reviewedAt`.
4. Run `npm run curriculum-gate` and keep the pathway at `mapped` until step 3 is done.
5. Only then move the learner-facing statement from “mapped” to “reviewed” or “verified”.

Until then, the product should say **“course coverage”**, not “syllabus verified”.

## 4. The formatting defect

The original report was exact: the lesson “shows up as a block of AI generated text”.
The cause was structural, not a font choice:

- a plan carried `idea: string[]`, and the page printed every paragraph into one card;
- a formula was prose (`n = m ÷ Mr` inside a sentence);
- a method was a sentence, not an ordered sequence;
- a comparison was a run-on, not a table;
- the vocabulary and data were flattened into the same visual weight as the explanation.

After the change, a learner sees: **numbered heading → short paragraphs → formula box →
table or steps → worked example → trap → recap**. The static twin uses the same
structure, and both stylesheets give it the same measure and spacing.

This does not make the prose “not AI-generated”; that is not a formatting property. It
makes the lesson **readable as a lesson**: a learner can find the formula, follow the
method and check the example. The remaining content risk is review, not layout.
