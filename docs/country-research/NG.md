# Nigeria (NG)

> **Research status: `INITIAL_RESEARCH`.** Nigeria is not one uniform school
> system. This profile records national and regional evidence and treats the
> state, school type and language as variables to establish, not as details to
> average away. It does not establish that OpenMind's JSS/SSS concepts match
> WAEC/NECO or any state syllabus, and no Nigerian teacher or subject specialist
> has reviewed the lessons.

## 1. National school structure

The registry records one national pathway, **Nigeria · JSS/SSS**, covering JSS 1–3
and SSS 1–3, with BECE and WAEC/NECO SSSCE. The 6-3-3-4 structure is a national
framework, not a guarantee that every state, private school, Islamiyya school or
non-formal centre follows it in the same way.

The registry does not model state ministries, examination boards, technical and
vocational routes, Quranic/Islamiyya education, nomadic education or non-formal
equivalency. Those omissions are limits of the route, not evidence that they do
not exist.

## 2. Curriculum

The national curriculum framework is not the same as a state's implemented
syllabus. The mathematics and science syllabi, the sequence of topics, the
practical requirements and the current revision dates have **not** been reviewed
in this pass.

The route is recorded as one national pathway, but a learner in Lagos, Kano or
Rivers may be taught a different sequence and depth. Mapping one national course
onto all Nigerian learners would repeat the defect this registry was built to
avoid.

## 3. Examinations

The registry records BECE and WAEC/NECO SSSCE. The examination formats, subject
combinations, practical papers, grading and state-level assessment requirements
have not been reviewed. No claim is made that OpenMind's assessment formats match
WAEC or NECO.

The regional range is the design signal: a national average of 27% reading does
not describe either the North West or the South East. OpenMind must not use one
Nigerian profile to set the language, examples or expected prior knowledge for
every learner.

## 5. Foundational skills

The MICS 2021 figures show that roughly three-quarters of 7–14-year-olds did not
demonstrate basic reading or numeracy. That is a foundational-learning problem
inside the school system, not only an access problem.

A JSS 1 learner may therefore be working several years below the nominal grade.
Placement must test prerequisites and must not permanently label the learner.

The SitAn describes crowded classrooms and insufficient teacher training. It does
not establish the subject knowledge of a particular teacher, the amount of
in-service support available, or how often teachers are absent. Those remain
unverified.

## 7. Classroom conditions

The evidence supports designing for **crowded, mixed-ability classes with unequal
resources across states**. The national ratio is not a class size, and the
northern/southern differences mean a single "Nigerian classroom" would be a
fiction.

No current national distribution of class size, double-shift schooling,
multigrade teaching or classroom materials has been collected here.

## 8. Textbook availability

No current national pupil-textbook ratio has been verified for Nigeria. The
practical design rule is therefore the safe one: a lesson cannot require a
textbook in every pupil's hands, and a worksheet cannot assume home printing.

## 9. Language policy

Nigeria is one of the most linguistically diverse countries in the world. English
is the official language and the language of national examinations; many pupils
learn in a home or community language that is not English. The National Policy on
Education has supported use of the child's mother tongue or the language of the
immediate community in early primary education, but implementation varies by
state, school type and community.

This profile has **not** verified the language policy of a particular state or the
language actually used in a particular classroom. It does not infer a pupil's
strongest language from the national language or the examination language.

## 10. Common teaching languages

English is the examination language and a major teaching language. Hausa, Yoruba,
Igbo and many other languages are used in homes, communities and early-primary
provision. The exact mix cannot be reduced to a national list.

OpenMind needs the learner or teacher to select the explanation language, and it
must keep the examination's subject terminology visible. A translation of the
interface is not a reviewed translation of the lesson.

## 11. Technology access

No current, nationally representative school-level internet or computer figure
has been verified in this pass. Household mobile access is not school
connectivity, and a school computer is not a pupil device.

The product must work with one teacher computer and no pupil devices, with shared
devices, and with no connection during the lesson.

## 12. Electricity

No current school electricity figure has been verified for Nigeria here. The
design baseline is a charged teacher device and an offline/printed lesson; the
lesson must not require mains power or a projector.

## 13. Internet

No current school internet figure has been verified. The lesson must not require
a live request, streaming video or a cloud service to be taught. Downloading and
printing must be possible while connected, and the saved lesson must work later.

## 14. Devices

No current personal pupil-device ownership figure has been collected. The
shared-device and no-device modes are both necessary; the product must not require
a personal account, a smartphone or a data bundle for a pupil to learn.

## 15. Gender and inclusion

The 2024 SitAn reports out-of-school and learning disparities by region, with
particular barriers for girls in parts of the north. The figures used here are
not a gender-disaggregated national series and do not establish the situation of
girls in a particular state.

Disability, language minorities, nomadic communities, displaced learners,
child labour and non-formal education remain unverified in this profile. They are
not optional edge cases in the Nigerian route.

## 16. Rural and urban differences

The North West/South East reading range and the Lagos outlier show that national
figures hide enormous differences. They do not establish a simple rural/urban
split: Lagos is urban and high-performing, but there are poor urban schools, and
not every rural school is low-performing.

OpenMind must ask about the actual school context rather than infer it from the
state or the country.

## 17. Offline requirements

Nigeria needs an offline-first path for schools with no reliable connection, plus
a shared-device path for schools with a small computer room. The essential
lesson, worksheet, teacher key and assessment must survive:

- no live internet during the lesson;
- no pupil account;
- a shared device or no device;
- a teacher who has limited preparation time;
- later synchronisation, not real-time dependence.

## 18. Cost constraints

Household costs for uniforms, transport, materials and data can exclude children
even where tuition is officially free. OpenMind must not add a mandatory personal
device, data purchase, home printing or paid subscription to the essential
learning path.

## 19. Relevant educational interventions

The evidence supports **foundational catch-up, structured pedagogy and
teacher-led large-class routines**. Teaching at the Right Level has evidence from
other low- and middle-income systems, including Ghana, but this pass did not
verify a Nigerian trial or effect size. No intervention is called proven in
Nigeria on the strength of another country's evaluation.

## 20. OpenMind product implications

1. **Do not build one generic Nigerian pathway.** State, examination board,
   language and school type are separate choices that must be established.
2. **Separate explanation language from examination language.** A pupil may need
   Hausa, Yoruba, Igbo or another language to understand the idea while needing
   English terminology for WAEC/NECO.
3. **Support non-formal and interrupted education.** A learner may not attend a
   conventional JSS/SSS school; the route must not assume one.
4. **Make large-class, no-device teaching first-class.** The presenter's
   large-class and no-device modes are directly relevant.
5. **Place by prerequisites.** JSS 1 is not a guarantee of primary mastery.
6. **Do not claim curriculum alignment.** The WAEC/NECO and state mappings remain
   unreviewed.

## 21. Unverified questions

- Which state curriculum, examination board and school type does a given learner
  actually follow?
- What is the current language-of-instruction policy and practice in that state,
  and what language does the pupil understand best?
- What are the actual class sizes, textbook ratios and school resources in rural
  and urban schools?
- How reliable is school electricity and internet during the school day?
- What is the distribution of non-formal, Islamiyya, nomadic and technical
  education, and how do learners move between them and the formal route?
- What are the gender, disability and displacement barriers in each region?
- Which examples, names, units and practical activities in OpenMind's lessons are
  unfamiliar or inappropriate in a given community?
- What would a teacher with 60+ pupils and no pupil devices actually use?
