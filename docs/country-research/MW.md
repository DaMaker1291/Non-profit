# Malawi (MW)

> **Research status: `INITIAL_RESEARCH`.** Malawi is a stress test for the
> product, not a claim that one school represents the country. This profile
> establishes severe material constraints from published sources. It does not
> establish that OpenMind's Form 1–4 concepts match the Malawian syllabus, and no
> Malawian teacher or subject specialist has reviewed the lessons.

## 1. National school structure

The registry records one national pathway, **Malawi · Secondary**, covering
Forms 1–4 and ending in the MSCE, awarded by the Malawi National Examinations
Board (MANEB), with JCE and MSCE examinations.

Primary schooling, early childhood education, community day secondary schools,
open and distance learning, and non-formal pathways are not modelled by the
registry. A learner in Form 1 may have had an interrupted primary education; the
school year does not establish mastery.

## 2. Curriculum

The registry records the secondary structure, not the subject syllabus. The
primary curriculum, the secondary mathematics and science syllabi, practical
requirements and recent revisions have **not** been reviewed in this pass.

The existence of a national route is not evidence that OpenMind's 135 concepts
match the Malawian curriculum.

## 3. Examinations

The registry records JCE and MSCE under MANEB. The paper structure, subject
combinations, practical examinations, grading and school-based assessment have
not been reviewed. No claim is made that OpenMind's assessment formats mirror
MANEB's.

## 5. Foundational skills

The 19% reading and 13% numeracy figures are the clearest signal in this profile:
a large majority of 7–14-year-olds do not demonstrate foundational skills. OpenMind
must therefore make prerequisites visible and teachable, not assume that a
secondary lesson can begin at secondary level.

The figures are from 2019/20 and are not a measurement of the current cohort. No
current national learning assessment has been reviewed here.

A lesson cannot require a textbook in every pupil's hands, and a worksheet cannot
assume every pupil can print one. Shared reading, board work and exercise books
must be first-class.

## 9. Language policy

Malawi is multilingual. Chichewa is a national language and English is the
official language and a core school subject. The exact language of instruction by
grade, the treatment of other local languages, and the transition into
English-medium secondary subjects have **not** been verified in this pass.

This profile does not claim that every Malawian pupil is fluent in Chichewa or
English, or that a national policy describes a particular classroom.

## 10. Common teaching languages

Chichewa and English are the languages most relevant to schooling in the sources
reviewed here. Other languages are spoken in homes and communities. A pupil's
strongest language must be asked for, not inferred from the country or the school.

## 11. Technology access

No current, nationally representative school-level internet or computer figure
has been collected for Malawi in this pass. Sub-Saharan Africa's regional
electricity and connectivity deficits cannot be used as a Malawian school
statistic.

The design assumption is therefore the safe one: **do not assume a computer room,
a projector, pupil devices or a live connection**.

## 12. Electricity

No country-specific school electricity figure has been verified. The product must
not require mains power during a lesson: a printed pack, a charged teacher device
and an offline lesson are the resilient baseline.

## 13. Internet

No country-specific school internet figure has been verified. Household mobile
coverage is not school connectivity. The lesson must remain usable after a
download or print, with no background request needed to teach it.

## 14. Devices

No current pupil-device ownership figure has been collected. The existing
shared-device and no-device modes are both necessary; neither can be treated as
the only Malawian experience.

## 15. Gender and inclusion

The 2024/25 budget brief reports lower secondary completion for girls than boys
(20.6% versus 22.3% in the table reviewed). That is a national signal, not an
explanation. Disability, language, rural poverty, child labour, pregnancy and
re-entry are not resolved by a single completion gap and remain unverified here.

## 16. Rural and urban differences

The sources reviewed describe national and school-level averages, not a rural/urban
comparison. Malawi's rural majority and the concentration of disadvantage in
remote areas are plausible design concerns, but this profile does not claim a
specific rural class size, teacher ratio or connectivity figure.

## 17. Offline requirements

Malawi is the clearest case for **print-first, offline-first delivery**:

- the teacher must be able to teach from a printed or locally stored lesson;
- the pupil worksheet and separate key must be printable at school;
- the lesson must not depend on a live video or a cloud call;
- a shared textbook must not be required for every pupil;
- assessment must work on paper and be enterable later;
- the teacher must be able to prepare with limited time and no reliable internet.

## 18. Cost constraints

The 2024/25 brief reports constrained education funding and limited teaching and
learning materials. OpenMind must not impose hidden costs on a school or family:
no required personal device, no mandatory data purchase, no assumption of
home printing, and no design that requires a paid subscription to access the
essential lesson.

## 19. Relevant educational interventions

The evidence points to **structured, teacher-led lessons with plenty of oral
practice and shared materials**, plus foundational grouping. Teaching at the Right
Level and catch-up models are relevant, but this pass did not verify a Malawian
trial or effect size. No intervention is recommended as proven in Malawi on the
strength of evidence from another country.

## 20. OpenMind product implications

1. **Print-first is the primary path**, not a fallback. Every lesson needs a
   worksheet, a separate key and a teacher run sheet that works from paper.
2. **One teacher, 90+ pupils** must be a supported mode. The presenter now has a
   large-class mode with shoulder partners, response cards and sampled oral
   answers.
3. **Shared textbooks and no laboratory** must be assumed. The limited-materials
   mode must describe safe substitutes and never pretend apparatus was used.
4. **Chichewa/English separation** must be explicit in the language profile; a
   translation of the interface is not a reviewed lesson translation.
5. **Foundational placement** must be available before Form 1 content. A pupil
   who cannot handle fractions needs a short prerequisite route, not a label.
6. **No curriculum-alignment claim** until a Malawian subject specialist has read
   the mapping.

## 21. Unverified questions

- What is the current Form 1–4 mathematics and science syllabus, and what
  practical work is required?
- What are the actual language-of-instruction rules by grade, and how do teachers
  use Chichewa, English and community languages in practice?
- What is the distribution of class size and multigrade teaching, not just the
  national pupil-teacher ratio?
- How many pupils share a textbook in each subject, and how is sharing organised?
- How reliable is electricity and connectivity in rural schools during the school
  day?
- Which learners are out of school, over-age, returning after interruption or in
  non-formal education?
- What assistive resources exist for learners with disabilities?
- Which examples and practical activities in OpenMind's current lessons are
  impossible or unsafe in a Malawian classroom?
- What would a teacher with no preparation time actually use from a printed pack?
