Equal groups → multiplication

Ages 6–10 · 40 minutes · one teacher, any class size · no devices, no projector, no printing required

Unreviewed draft. No teacher has checked this card. Teach it, then answer the only question that matters: could you teach this tomorrow with what your school has? Mark anything that is wrong, too slow, or likely to be misread.

Before class — three things only

  1. Objects or none? One set per row, or fingers and chalk marks. Decide now, not in front of the class. If you use objects, plan the sentence that links each one to the idea — objects handed out without that link teach much less than objects explained.
  2. Write phase 2 on the board before pupils arrive. Four lines. Writing while sixty pupils wait is where lessons die.
  3. Fix the pairs and pick six to watch — two likely to struggle, two middle, two fast. You are not marking sixty books.

The lesson

1. Introduce — 5 min

Twelve stones in a mess on the ledge, spread out. “How many? How did you count them?” Take three answers before confirming. Then group the same twelve into three clear groups of four. “Did I add any? Did I take any away?” No. Same total, different arrangement.

Board: 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 beside 3 groups of 4.

2. Demonstrate — 10 min

5 + 5 = 10  →  2 × 5 = 10
2 + 2 + 2 + 2 = 8  →  4 × 2 = 8
4 + 4 + 4 = 12  →  3 × 4 = 12

“The first number tells you how many groups. The second tells you how many in each group.”

Then 4 × 3: “Why did the total go up by four, not by one?” — one more in each of four groups is four more. Give it thirty seconds of silence.

Double count — say both numbers. “One group of four, two groups of four, three groups of four — twelve.” A pupil who counts only the stones has counted; a pupil who counts the groups and the stones has started multiplicative thinking. Repeated addition alone leaves an additive model that fails later with fractions, scaling and × 0. This sentence is the cheapest correction in the lesson.

3. Guided practice — 10 min

Pairs build, then write or say: 3 groups of 6 · 5 groups of 2 · 2 × 7. Walk and look at six pairs only; their errors decide phase 5.

4. Independent practice — 10 min

Draw 4 groups of 3 · write 2+2+2+2+2 as a multiplication · 6 × 2 · a bench has 4 legs, how many legs on 5 benches? · (hard) I have 12 stones and put 3 in each group — how many groups?

Answers: 3+3+3+3=12, 4×3=12 · 5×2=10 · 12 · 20 · 4 groups

5. Check understanding — 5 min

Whole class, thirty seconds: “Show me on your fingers: 3 × 5.” Count the wrong and the silent — that number, not the individual, is what you act on. Then three written items, books closed, from your six sampled pupils only.

Three things not to say

  • “Multiplication is just adding.” It is the door, not the definition — it fails at 0 × 4 and at scaling. Say: “we can write it as adding, and we can also just know it.”
  • “Multiplication always makes the number bigger.” False at × 0, × 1 and fractions. Say: “with these numbers, the total grows.”
  • Two different words for “groups of”. One term, the one in the textbook, for the whole lesson.

Boxes 1, 2 and 4 are the objective. Box 5 is the misconception. Box 6 is transfer. A pupil who scores 5 but not 1 has a writing or copying problem, not a mathematical one — check it aloud.

Tomorrow — decide before you mark

  • Most wrong on the fingers check: reteach equal groups with objects. Not arrays.
  • Right answers, can’t explain: ten minutes on what each number means before new content.
  • Most can explain box 5 and the “is 4 × 3 = 7?” item: move to arrays and 3 × 4 = 4 × 3.

One line home

“Ask someone at home to make three groups of four stones with you, and tell them how many.”

No worksheet, no reading, no cost, one minute, any language. Not homework. Do not mark it.

If it falls apart

  • 20 minutes only: phases 1, 2 and 5. The lesson still works.
  • No objects: board groups, fingers, chalk marks on the desk. Say plainly that a drawing records the idea; it does not replace handling objects.
  • Objects thrown: that pair works on the board. Remove the objects, never the mathematics.
  • Half the class away: do not reteach. Open with the six boxes and carry on. Record who missed what — that record is the finding.
  • If you are unsure of the maths: you do not need to be confident. You need to be willing to let the stones contradict you. If a pupil asks something you cannot answer, say so and write it on the board.

Equal groups → multiplication, step 1 of 8. Steps 2–8 (arrays, the 2/5/10 tables, commutativity, 3 and 4 tables, sharing, one-step problems, missing numbers) are named, not written. This card is unreviewed and unvalidated; it is evidence of nothing until a teacher has taught it.

Where the design comes from. Structured pedagogy (lesson plans + materials + ongoing teacher support) is one of three “Great Buys” in the 2023 GEEAP Smart Buys report, with evidence from Kenya, Liberia and South Africa; the same report rates computer hardware a “Bad Buy”, which is why this card needs none. Oral, individually administered assessment is the design of the Early Grade Mathematics Assessment (EGMA), built specifically so that a child’s reading cannot be mistaken for their mathematics. The double count follows research on multiplicative reasoning, which finds that teaching multiplication only as repeated addition leaves a model that fails with non-whole numbers. Manipulatives are supported by the evidence when the adult makes the link explicit, which is why phase 3 is “build, then say, then write” and never “here are some stones”.
