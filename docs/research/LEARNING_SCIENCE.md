# Learning science: what the evidence supports, and what it does not

**Purpose.** This is the evidence base for the OpenMind lesson-quality framework.
It separates findings that are strong enough to design around from findings that
are promising but uncertain, and it records the limits that matter for a digital
lesson.

**A warning about effect sizes.** An average effect is not a guarantee for a
particular lesson. The studies below vary in population, subject, duration and
outcome measure. Where a number is reported, it is the number the cited review
reports, not a prediction for OpenMind.

## At a glance

| Domain | Strength | Design implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retrieval practice | Strong for retention; moderate for classroom transfer | Short, low-stakes recall before and after teaching; feedback; a second attempt |
| Spaced practice | Strong in laboratory; moderate in classrooms; weak within one lesson | Schedule revisits after days, weeks and a month; do not call end-of-lesson repetition “spacing” |
| Worked examples and fading | Moderate–strong for maths/science novices; weak for a precise fading schedule | Complete example → completion problem → independent problem; remove support as success grows |
| Cognitive load | Strong theory; moderate applied evidence | One task at a time; remove decorative load; keep labels near diagrams; segment processes |
| Formative assessment and feedback | Strong that feedback can work; variable and sometimes harmful | Task/process feedback, a diagnostic distractor, a required action after feedback |
| Explicit instruction / gradual release | Moderate–strong for components; weak for the full package as a unique method | Model thinking; check understanding; guided practice; independent practice |
| Conceptual understanding / multiple representations | Moderate | Connect symbols, diagrams and words; use two well-mapped representations, not five decorative ones |
| Transfer | Weak for far transfer; moderate for near transfer | Teach a transferable routine and assess an unfamiliar context; do not assume familiar practice transfers |
| Metacognition | Moderate–strong when embedded in a subject | Plan–monitor–evaluate inside the task, not as a generic study-skills lesson |
| Motivation | Moderate correlational; weak–moderate causal | Competence, autonomy, belonging and visible progress; avoid manipulative streaks |
| Misconception-based teaching | Moderate–strong in science; weaker for durability | Name the belief, explain why it fails, provide a contrast pair, probe it again later |
| Subject-specific pedagogy | Strong as a curriculum/assessment requirement; variable as a causal comparison | Match the evidence task to the discipline; do not use one template for all subjects |

## 1. Retrieval practice

**What the evidence says.** Recalling information from memory improves later
retention more than rereading. A review of 50 classroom experiments found
positive effects across ages and subjects, with many medium or large effects
(Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan, 2017). Karpicke and Blunt (2011) found that
retrieval practice improved comprehension and inference compared with concept
mapping, even when the final test was a concept map.

**Limits.** Much of the evidence uses researcher-designed tests and short
retention intervals. A large share of classroom experiments were conducted
outside the countries where most learners live. The evidence for complex
transfer is weaker than for retention of taught content. Retrieval is not
automatically good: a quiz that rewards guessing, or that only recognises an
answer, is not the same as cued recall.

**Implication for OpenMind.** Put a short prerequisite check before the teaching
and a retrieval check after it, then schedule cumulative revisits. Ask the
learner to produce an answer or explanation before revealing one. A multiple-
choice item can be useful, but it must not be the only evidence.

## 2. Spaced practice

**What the evidence says.** Distributing practice over time produces better
long-term retention than massing it. Cepeda et al. (2006) synthesised 317
experiments and found that the optimal gap increases with the retention interval;
there is no single best schedule. The EEF's cognitive-science review found
positive but variable classroom effects, and very few studies of spacing within
a single lesson.

**Limits.** The laboratory literature is mostly verbal recall; classroom studies
are fewer and often researcher-delivered. “Spacing” is not the same as
“interleaving”, and interleaving has narrower evidence. A timetable can make a
well-designed schedule impossible.

**Implication for OpenMind.** Build retrieval into the course map, not only the
lesson page: next day, week and month. Store the schedule with the learner's
record, and show the learner why a previously taught idea is returning. Do not
claim that three questions at the end of one lesson are spaced practice.

## 3. Worked examples and fading

**What the evidence says.** For novices in well-structured domains, studying a
worked example can be more efficient than immediately solving equivalent
problems. Barbieri et al.'s meta-analysis of mathematics studies reported a
positive average effect (reported g ≈ 0.48), and the EEF review found supporting
classroom evidence mostly in maths and science. Correct examples were more
effective than incorrect examples alone in that meta-analysis.

**Limits.** Heterogeneity is high, most outcomes are short-term accuracy, and
fading has far less evidence than the initial worked-example effect. Expertise
reversal means support that helps a novice can become redundant or harmful for a
more capable learner. A worked example that states the steps without the reasons
is a procedure demonstration, not an explanation.

**Implication for OpenMind.** Every worked example must name **why** each move is
valid. Sequence: complete example → completion problem → independent problem.
Fade according to success, not a fixed timetable. Do not add a second
representation or a self-explanation prompt unless it is doing specific work.

## 4. Cognitive load

**What the evidence says.** Working memory is limited; novices learn better when
unnecessary load is reduced and relevant information is presented coherently.
The EEF review supports worked examples, scaffolds and guidance, especially for
novices, but describes the applied evidence as promising rather than definitive.
Spatial contiguity (labels near the part of the diagram they describe),
segmenting a complex process and removing redundant text are well-supported
instructional techniques.

**Limits.** Cognitive load is not directly observable. “Minimise load” is a
misreading: the aim is to optimise it, preserving the productive difficulty of
the subject. Decorative images, autoplay and split attention add extraneous load.

**Implication for OpenMind.** One teaching move per section. Keep a formula next
to the substitution that uses it. Put diagram labels on the diagram. Segment a
multi-stage process rather than presenting it as one wall. Remove a visual that
does not make a relationship visible.

## 5. Formative assessment and feedback

**What the evidence says.** Feedback can have a large average impact, but it is
highly variable. The EEF estimates about six additional months of progress on
average, with digital feedback somewhat lower; Wisniewski et al.'s meta-analysis
reported a moderate average effect but also a substantial minority of negative
effects. Kluger and DeNisi found that over one-third of feedback interventions
reduced performance. Task-focused and self-regulatory feedback is generally more
effective than praise or grades alone.

**Limits.** “Feedback” is not one treatment. The EEF's Embedding Formative
Assessment trial in 140 secondary schools found a small, marginally significant
average effect and no clear improvement in GCSE English or maths. A wrong-answer
message is not formative assessment unless it changes what the learner does next.

**Implication for OpenMind.** Use hinge questions and diagnostic distractors.
Name the likely misconception, give a hint that moves one step, and require the
learner to retry a fresh item. Do not show a grade alone. Do not mark a
constructed response with a vocabulary-matching score and call it understanding.

## 6. Explicit instruction and gradual release

**What the evidence says.** Clear explanation, modelling, guided practice,
checking for understanding and independent practice are supported by a substantial
evidence base, including Direct Instruction studies and syntheses of explicit
instruction. Rosenshine's principles are a useful synthesis, not a single
meta-analysis. The IES writing guide recommends explicit strategy instruction
with modelling and gradual release.

**Limits.** The full “I do, we do, you do” package has not been tested as a
single intervention in a large meta-analysis; the evidence is stronger for its
components. Explicit instruction can become a long lecture with no checks, or
scaffolding that never fades.

**Implication for OpenMind.** Model the thinking, not just the answer. Check
understanding before independent practice. Branch: if the check fails, reteach or
scaffold; if it succeeds, fade. Do not use discovery for a novice who lacks the
knowledge to discover with.

## 7. Conceptual understanding and multiple representations

**What the evidence says.** Conceptual and procedural knowledge develop together;
connecting them is more effective than teaching either in isolation. Multiple
representations can help when the mapping between them is explicit. The IES guide
gives moderate evidence to combining graphics with verbal descriptions and
connecting abstract and concrete representations.

**Limits.** “Multiple representations” can become a cognitive-load problem if
the learner has to work out how the representations relate. A diagram is not
self-explanatory; it needs a caption that states the relationship.

**Implication for OpenMind.** Use a representation because it makes a specific
relationship visible, and say what the learner should notice. Connect the
equation to the diagram and to the situation in words. Do not add a picture for
decoration.

## 8. Transfer

**What the evidence says.** Near transfer is achievable; far transfer is much
harder and often not demonstrated. Familiar practice can create an illusion of
understanding because the learner recognises the format rather than selecting
the idea. Transfer improves when learners practice choosing a method across
varied contexts and when the underlying structure is made explicit.

**Limits.** Most studies use researcher-designed transfer tasks and short
follow-up. A single “challenge question” at the end of a lesson is weak evidence
of transfer.

**Implication for OpenMind.** End with an unfamiliar context that requires the
learner to choose the concept, not merely substitute new numbers. Ask them to
name why the same reasoning applies. Test transfer after a delay, not
immediately after the explanation.

## 9. Metacognition

**What the evidence says.** Planning, monitoring and evaluating learning can
improve outcomes when they are embedded in a subject and modelled by a teacher.
Generic “study skills” lessons have weaker evidence. Confidence is not a reliable
measure of correctness.

**Limits.** Self-report is easy to fake and poorly calibrated, especially for
novices. A confidence slider is not mastery evidence.

**Implication for OpenMind.** Ask “what did you assume?”, “which step would you
check?”, and “how do you know?” inside the worked example and the practice
feedback. Do not award mastery for a self-rating.

## 10. Motivation and engagement

**What the evidence says.** Competence, autonomy and belonging are associated
with motivated persistence. Visible progress and meaningful feedback can support
persistence. Gamification can increase engagement without increasing learning;
streaks and points can even displace the learning goal.

**Limits.** Much of the evidence is correlational, and engagement metrics are
easily mistaken for learning. A learner who completes more items is not
necessarily learning more.

**Implication for OpenMind.** Show what the learner can now do, not only a score.
Give choice where it does not remove essential practice. Do not reward opening a
video or clicking a hint as if it were evidence of understanding.

## 11. Misconception-based teaching

**What the evidence says.** In science, eliciting and confronting a misconception
can improve conceptual change, especially when the learner sees why the wrong
model fails. The evidence is stronger for immediate conceptual outcomes than for
durable transfer. Naming a misconception without a contrast or a repair is a
label, not teaching.

**Limits.** Many studies are small and short. A misconception probe can
inadvertently reinforce the wrong idea if the correct model is not made salient.

**Implication for OpenMind.** Every misconception should have: the belief in the
learner's words, why it fails, the correct reasoning, and a contrast pair. Probe
it again after a delay. Do not score a learner's explanation by counting lesson
vocabulary.

## 12. Subject-specific pedagogy

**What the evidence says.** Curriculum and assessment requirements differ by
discipline. Mathematics requires reasoning, representation and problem-solving,
not only arithmetic. Science requires models, evidence, units and experimental
reasoning. Computing requires reading, writing, tracing, testing and debugging
real code, not only vocabulary. English requires close reading and purposeful
writing. Geography requires maps, data and fieldwork. History requires
substantive knowledge and evidence-based disciplinary reasoning.

**Limits.** These are curriculum and subject-community claims, not a single
causal experiment. A generic lesson template can still provide useful routines,
but it must not dictate the intellectual task.

**Implication for OpenMind.** The subject frameworks in `lib/lesson-frameworks.ts`
are the right integration point: maths needs a worked expression, physics needs
units and an equation, chemistry needs a balanced equation and particle model,
biology needs structure–function and process, computing needs code, trace and
test. A lesson that cannot meet its subject framework is not complete.

## What this means for OpenMind

The evidence supports a specific lesson shape, not a longer page:

1. **Check the prerequisite** and branch if it is missing.
2. **Hook with a concrete case**, before the terminology.
3. **Explain the idea in small, connected sections**, each with a reason.
4. **Model one example completely**, naming why each move is valid.
5. **Fade the support**: a completion problem, then an independent problem.
6. **Probe a misconception** with a contrast, then repair it.
7. **Assess with a task that requires explanation or application**, not only a
   click.
8. **Give task-level feedback** and require a retry on a fresh item.
9. **Transfer to an unfamiliar context.**
10. **Retrieve after a delay**, with a schedule.

This is the standard the authored-lesson contract already encodes. The work
ahead is to author the lessons, not to add another dashboard.
